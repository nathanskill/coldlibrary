In the morning the Master of the National Academy came into the Imperial College, called the students to stand below the hall, and instructed them: “Learning is perfected by diligence and wasted by play; conduct is formed by thought and spoiled by drifting. Now sage rulers and worthy ministers have met, and every instrument of government has been set in place. The wicked have been rooted out, and the able raised to high office. Whoever has the smallest merit is put on the list; whoever is known for a single skill is employed. They are combed through and sifted out, scraped of their grime and polished to brightness. Some, perhaps, are chosen by luck; but who can say that a man of many gifts is passed over? Students, worry that your learning is not refined, not that the officials are not clear-sighted; worry that your conduct is not formed, not that the officials are not fair.”

Before he had finished, someone in the ranks laughed and said: “Master, you are deceiving us. We have served you for some years now. Your mouth never stops reciting the texts of the Six Arts, and your hand never stops turning the volumes of the hundred schools. When you record events you always pick out the essentials; when you compile sayings you always draw out what is hidden in them. Greedy for more and set on getting it, you throw nothing away, great or small; you burn the lamp oil to carry on after the sun, and toil on unresting through the years. Master, your labour can be called diligent. You have stood against heresy and driven out the Buddhists and the Daoists; you have patched the cracks and gaps and brought what was dim and remote into the light; you have sought the broken threads of the tradition where they were lost in vagueness, and searched alone, far and wide, to join them up again; you have dammed the hundred rivers and turned them east, and turned back the wild waves after they had broken. Master, for the Confucian way you can be said to have toiled. Steeped in its rich fragrance, savouring its flowers and chewing its blossoms, you have written essays until your books fill the house. Above, you take as your models the vast and boundless documents of Yu and Shun; the Zhou proclamations and the Yin addresses, knotted and hard on the tongue; the Spring and Autumn, strict and severe; the Zuo commentary, ornate and extravagant; the Changes, strange yet lawful; the Songs, upright and in flower. Below, you reach down to Zhuangzi and the Li Sao, to what the Grand Historian recorded, to Ziyun and Xiangru, who played different tunes with the same skill. Master, in literature you can be said to be broad within and unconstrained without. Young, you began to learn and were bold in action; grown, you understood the methods and were fit for anything. Master, as a man you can be said to be complete. And yet in public you are not trusted by others, and in private you are not helped by your friends. You trip going forward and stumble going back, and every move brings blame. You were a censor for a moment and were promptly banished among the southern tribes; three years a professor, you have been idle, with nothing to govern. Your fate conspires with your enemies, and defeat is never far away. In a mild winter your children cry with cold; in a rich harvest your wife weeps with hunger. Your head is bald and your teeth have gaps; what good will it do you to go on like this until you die? Instead of thinking about that, you turn round and teach others?”

The Master said: “Ah, come here. Great timbers for beams and small timbers for rafters, brackets and short posts, door pivots and doorstops, bolts and door posts, each in its proper place and put together into a house: that is the craft of the carpenter. Burnet and cinnabar, red arrow and green fungus, plantain and puffballs, the skin of a broken drum, all gathered up and stored together, waiting to be used, none left out: that is the excellence of the physician. Promoting with clear sight and choosing with fairness, bringing in the skilful and the clumsy together, valuing the gentle for their grace and the outstanding for their boldness, comparing short and long and using each according to its capacity: that is the method of the chief minister. Long ago Meng Ke loved to argue, and the way of Confucius was made clear by him; his carriage tracks circled the empire, and he grew old on the road. Xun Qing held to what was right and spread his great arguments wide; he fled slander by going to Chu and died out of office at Lanling. These two scholars uttered words that became classics and took steps that became rules; they left their kind far behind and passed easily into the realm of the sages. And how did the world receive them? Now your master, though his study is diligent, does not keep to the line of the tradition; though his words are many, they do not reach the centre; though his writing is unusual, it is no practical help; though his conduct is careful, it does not stand out among the crowd. Even so he spends his monthly salary and uses up his yearly grain. His son does not know how to plough and his wife does not know how to weave; he rides a horse with followers behind him and eats sitting at his ease. He treads the common road with cramped little steps, and peers into old books to steal from them. And yet the sage ruler has not punished him, and the chief ministers have not dismissed him: is that not his good fortune? Whatever he does draws slander, and his name follows after it; that he has been set aside in an idle post is no more than his share. As for reckoning whether he has wealth or not and counting the height of his grade, forgetting what his own measure deserves and pointing at the faults of those above him: that is like scolding the carpenter for not using a stake as a pillar, or blaming the physician for prolonging life with calamus and wanting him to give you hog-tuber instead.”
