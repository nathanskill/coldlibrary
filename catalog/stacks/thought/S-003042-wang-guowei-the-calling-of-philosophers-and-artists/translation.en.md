There are things in the world that are most sacred and most noble and yet of no use to their own age: philosophy and art are these. Let the whole world clamour that they are useless; it takes nothing from the worth of philosophy and art. But when those who pursue them forget their sacred place and try to make them serve the uses of the age, both lose their worth. What philosophy and art aim at is truth. Truth is the truth of all the world and of ten thousand ages, not the truth of a single time. Whoever discovers that truth (the philosopher) or expresses it in signs (the artist) has done a work for all the world and for ten thousand ages, not for a single time. Because it is the truth of all the world and of ten thousand ages, it cannot always agree with the interests of one time and one country, and sometimes it cannot even live with them; and in this lies its sacredness. Moreover, of those the world calls useful, who is more useful than the statesman and the man of industry? People like to speak of usefulness, so let me speak of it too. What makes human beings different from the birds and beasts, if not that they have pure knowledge and subtle feeling? In the desire to live, people and beasts are no different. The statesman and the man of industry supply that desire; but the comfort and satisfaction of knowledge and feeling must be sought in philosophy and art. Judged by the nature of what they give to people, the two kinds of work are plainly unequal in worth. Judged by how far their effects reach, the work of the philosopher and the artist, a thousand years later and beyond the four seas, so long as the truth they discovered and the signs that express it remain, will satisfy and console human knowledge and feeling no less than it did at first; while the work of statesmen and men of industry rarely reaches five or ten generations. This is the difference between the lasting and the brief. So if a person contributes nothing to philosophy and art, so be it; but if he is a true philosopher or artist, why should he envy the statesman?

When I open the history of our Chinese philosophy, I find that every philosopher wanted to be a statesman as well, which is strange. Confucius was a great statesman, Mozi was a great statesman, and Mencius and Xunzi both cherished great political ambitions. So did Jia Yi and Dong Zhongshu of the Han; Zhang Zai, the Cheng brothers, Zhu Xi and Lu Jiuyuan of the Song; and Luo Qinshun and Wang Yangming of the Ming. And not only the philosophers: the poets too. "I thought I would rise at once and stand on the road to power, raise my lord above Yao and Shun, and make the customs pure again." Was this not the ambition of Du Fu? "Why not send up a memorial to recommend yourself, and make the four seas like the age of Yu and Tang?" Was this not the counsel of Han Yu? "Resigned to being laughed at for a thousand years in my loneliness, still I gallop on, hoping to see the lands by the two rivers made peaceful." Was this not the grief and anger of Lu You? Men like these are called great poets. As for poets without such ambitions, and writers of fiction and drama, painters and musicians, they all regard themselves as dwarfs and jesters, and the world keeps them as dwarfs and jesters. "There are more important things than poetry," and "Once a man is called a man of letters, there is nothing worth looking at in him": these are the golden rules of our countrymen. Alas, it has long been that art has no independent worth. No wonder that poets through the ages have so often excused themselves by pleading loyalty to the sovereign and love of the country, or the encouraging of good and punishing of evil, while writings of pure artistic purpose have often been persecuted by the world, with no one to clear their name. This is one of the reasons why philosophy and art have not flourished in our country.

So our country has no pure philosophy. The most complete it has are moral philosophy and political philosophy. The metaphysics of the Zhou and Qin and the two Song dynasties was only meant to strengthen the foundations of moral philosophy, and was not pursued out of any real interest in metaphysics for its own sake. If that was so even with metaphysics, how much more with aesthetics, logic, the theory of knowledge and such remote questions that nobody thinks urgent. Turning to poetry, we find the world of verse crammed with poems on history, on the past, on current events and on gifts to friends, while lyric and narrative works are not one in a hundred. What artistic worth it has lies in one side only: the portrayal of natural beauty. Even drama and fiction, which are pure literature, have often taken moral instruction as their aim, and those with a purely artistic purpose are not only unvalued by the world but even disparaged. In philosophy it is like this, in art like that. Is this only the fault of a world without discernment? Or is it also that philosophers and artists have forgotten their own sacred place and independent worth, and timidly submitted to the crowd?

There is another reason why our philosophers and poets have so often had political ambitions. The desire for power is something people are born with, and even sages and heroes cannot escape it. The greater a person's intellect, the stronger the desire. Those who are drawn to philosophy and art are always those of superior intellect, and so their desire for power is strong in proportion. Now that pure philosophy and pure art could gain no power in the thought of our country, where else could such people satisfy their desire for power but in politics? Besides, power in politics is visible and comes within one's own lifetime, while power in philosophy and art is invisible and comes after one is gone. So, unless one is a hero such as appears once in an age, few can resist the lure of power in their own time. And yet, is it not also that their love of philosophy and art was not deep enough, and they were not truly aware of their worth? When a person, after years and months of study, suddenly understands some truth of the universe and human life, or sets down in writing, painting or sculpture a vision once vague and impossible to grasp, this is the unfolding of the powers given him by nature, and the joy of that moment could never be exchanged for a throne. And so long as the universe and human life remain as they are, the power and worth of the truth he has discovered and expressed will remain as they are. These two things are reward enough for the philosopher and the artist. To forget the sacredness of philosophy and art and use them as tools of morality or politics is exactly what makes a work worthless. May the philosophers and artists to come never forget their calling, nor lose their independent place. That would be fortunate indeed.
