When I was young I loved learning. My family was poor and had no way of getting books to read, so I would borrow them from families who kept libraries, copy them out by hand, and return them on the day agreed. When the weather was bitterly cold, the ink-stone froze hard and my fingers would not bend or straighten, but I did not slacken. When I had finished copying, I hurried to return the book, never daring to go even a little past the agreed time. Because of this, many people lent me their books, and so I was able to read a great many. When I had come of age I longed all the more for the way of the sages and worthies, but I worried that I had no great teachers or famous men to study with. Once I walked more than a hundred miles to consult an accomplished scholar of my region, holding a classic and asking questions. The scholar was of high virtue and great reputation, his rooms were crowded with disciples, and he never softened his words or his look. I stood at his side, put forward my doubts and asked about principles, bending forward and inclining my ear to ask. Sometimes he scolded me, and then I became more respectful and more courteous, not daring to say a word in reply. When he was in a good mood again, I asked once more. So, foolish as I was, I did in the end come to learn something.

When I went to study with my teacher, I carried my book box on my back and walked in worn-out shoes through deep mountains and great valleys. In the depths of winter the wind was fierce, the snow was several feet deep, and the skin of my feet cracked without my noticing. When I reached the lodging house my limbs were stiff and would not move. A servant brought hot water and poured it over me and wrapped me in a quilt, and only after a long time did I warm up. At the inn the host provided two meals a day, with nothing fresh or rich to enjoy. My fellow students all wore embroidered silks, caps decorated with tassels of pearls and jewels, and white jade rings at their waists, with swords on their left and scent bags on their right, glittering like gods. I lived among them in a padded gown and worn clothes, without the slightest envy. Because there was something within that I took pleasure in, I did not notice that I had less to eat and wear than others. This is how diligent and how hard it was for me. Now, though I am old and have achieved nothing, I have the good fortune to be counted among the gentlemen, to receive the favour of the Son of Heaven, to follow behind the high ministers, to attend him every day and answer his questions, and to have my name mistakenly praised within the four seas. How much more could be achieved by those whose talents surpass mine?

Now you students learn in the Imperial Academy. The government gives you daily provisions, and your parents send you furs and linens each year, so you need not fear cold or hunger. You sit in great halls reciting the Songs and the Documents, without the toil of running about. You have directors and erudites as your teachers, and there is no question that goes unanswered, nothing you seek that you cannot find. All the books you should have are gathered here, and you need not copy them out by hand or borrow them from others before you can see them, as I did. If your studies are not refined or your virtue not complete, it is not that your natural gifts are poor, but that your heart is not as single-minded as mine was. How can it be anyone else's fault?

Ma Junze of Dongyang has been at the Imperial Academy for two years, and his fellows speak highly of his worth. When I came to the capital to attend court, he called on me as the son of a fellow townsman, and wrote me a long letter as his introductory gift. Its language was fluent and clear. When I discussed things with him, his words were gentle and his manner easy. He says that when he was young he worked very hard at his studies. He can be called someone who is good at learning. As he is about to go home to see his parents, I have told him about the hardships of learning. To say that I am encouraging a young man from my home town to learn is to understand my purpose. To accuse me of boasting about my good fortune and putting on airs before him: does that show any understanding of me?
