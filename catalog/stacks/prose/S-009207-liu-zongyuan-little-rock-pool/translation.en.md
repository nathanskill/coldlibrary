A hundred and twenty paces west of the little knoll, through a screen of bamboo, I heard the sound of water, like the chime of jade pendants at a belt, and my heart was glad of it. We cut a way through the bamboo and saw below us a little pool, its water especially clear and cold. Its bed is one whole rock. Near the bank the rock curls up out of the bottom to make shoals and islets, crags and ledges. Green trees and emerald creepers cover it, tangle, sway and hang, uneven, trailing in the wind.

There may be a hundred fish in the pool, and they all seem to swim in empty air with nothing to hold them up. The sunlight strikes straight to the bottom, and their shadows lie spread on the stones, quite still; then all at once they dart far off, coming and going in a flash, as if they were sharing the visitors’ pleasure.

Looking south-west down the pool, the stream turns like the stars of the Dipper and winds like a snake, now bright, now hidden. Its banks are as jagged as a dog’s teeth, and no one can tell where it rises.

I sat by the pool. Bamboo and trees closed in on every side; it was silent, with no one about; a chill went into my spirit and my bones, quiet, deep and sorrowful. Because the place was too pure and cold to stay in for long, I wrote this down and went away.

Those who came with me were Wu Wuling, Gong Gu and my younger brother Zongxuan. Following as attendants were two young men of the Cui family, Shuji and Fengyi.
