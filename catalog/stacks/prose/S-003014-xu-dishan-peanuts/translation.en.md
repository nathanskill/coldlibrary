Behind our house there was half a mu of empty ground. Mother said, "It's a pity to let it lie waste. Since you all love peanuts so much, let's turn it into a peanut garden." We brothers and sisters and the few little maids were all delighted. Some bought the seed, some dug the soil, some watered the beds, and in a few months, to our surprise, we had a harvest.

Mother said, "Tonight we can hold a harvest festival, and invite your father to taste our new peanuts. How about it?" We all agreed. Mother made the peanuts into several kinds of food, and said the festival was to be held in the thatched pavilion in the garden.

The weather that evening was not very good, but Father came too, which was really rare. Father said, "Do you like peanuts?"

We all answered at once, "Yes."

"Who can tell me what is good about peanuts?"

My elder sister said, "Peanuts taste very good."

My elder brother said, "Peanuts can be pressed for oil."

I said, "Anyone at all can buy them cheaply, and everyone likes to eat them. That is what is good about them."

Father said, "Peanuts certainly have many uses, but there is one thing about them that is very precious. This small nut is not like the handsome apple, peach or pomegranate, which hang their fruit on the branches, bright red and fresh green, so that whoever sees them envies them at once. It buries its fruit in the ground, and only when the fruit is ripe does it let people dig it up. If you happen to see a peanut plant growing timidly on the ground, you cannot tell at once whether it has any fruit or not. You can only know when you touch it."

We all said, "That's true." Mother nodded too. Father went on: "So you should be like the peanut, because it is useful, not great and handsome." I said, "Then people should be useful, and not great and grand." Father said, "That is what I hope for you."

We talked late into the night before we parted. All the peanut dishes were gone, but Father's words are still printed on the tablet of my heart.
