These past few days my heart has been far from calm. Tonight, sitting in the yard to enjoy the cool, I suddenly thought of the lotus pond I pass every day. Under the light of this full moon, it must look quite different. The moon had gradually risen higher, and the laughter of the children on the road beyond the wall could no longer be heard. In the house my wife was patting Run'er and humming a lullaby drowsily. Quietly I put on a long gown, pulled the door to behind me, and went out.

Along the lotus pond runs a winding path of cinders. It is a secluded path, with few people on it even in the daytime, and at night it is lonelier still. Around the pond grow many trees, dense and luxuriant. On one side of the path are willows, and some trees whose names I do not know. On nights without a moon the path is gloomy and a little frightening. But tonight it was very pleasant, though the moonlight was still faint.

I was alone on the path, strolling with my hands behind my back. This expanse of sky and earth seemed to be mine, and I seemed to have gone beyond my ordinary self into another world. I like a lively crowd, and I like stillness too; I like company, and I like solitude too. On a night like this, alone under the vast moon, I can think of anything, or think of nothing, and I feel like a free man. All the things I must do in the daytime and all the things I must say can be disregarded now. This is the beauty of solitude, and I will simply enjoy the boundless fragrance of the lotus and the light of the moon.

Over the winding pond, as far as the eye could see, were the leaves, round and full. They stood high out of the water, like the flared skirts of slender dancers. Among the layers of leaves white flowers were scattered here and there, some opening gracefully, some still shyly in bud, like pearls one by one, or stars in a blue sky, or beauties just out of the bath. A breeze passed, bringing wisps of fragrance, like a faint song from a distant tower. At such moments the leaves and flowers trembled a little, and the tremor passed like lightning across to the other side of the pond. The leaves had stood close together shoulder to shoulder, and now there was a ripple of deep jade green. Under the leaves the water flowed tenderly, hidden from sight so that no colour could be seen, and the leaves looked all the more graceful for it.

The moonlight poured down like flowing water, quietly, onto the leaves and flowers. A thin blue mist rose from the pond. The leaves and flowers seemed to have been washed in milk, or wrapped in a dream of gauze. Although the moon was full, there was a thin layer of cloud across the sky, so it could not shine brightly; but I thought this was just right, for while deep sleep is indispensable, a light doze has a charm of its own. The moonlight came through the trees. The bushes growing high up cast jagged, mottled black shadows, sharp and rugged like ghosts; the sparse, graceful shadows of the curving willows seemed to be painted on the lotus leaves. The moonlight on the pond was not even, but light and shadow made a harmonious melody, like a famous tune played on a violin.

All round the lotus pond, far and near, high and low, are trees, most of them willows. They surround the pond layer upon layer, and only on the side of the path are there a few gaps, as though left specially for the moonlight. The colour of the trees was all a deep shade, and at first glance they looked like a mass of mist, but even in the mist the grace of the willows could be made out. Above the treetops, faintly, was a line of distant hills, only an outline. Through the gaps between the trees a street lamp or two showed, listless, like the eyes of someone longing for sleep. The liveliest sounds at this hour were the cicadas in the trees and the frogs in the water; but the liveliness was theirs, and I had nothing.

Suddenly I thought of lotus gathering. Lotus gathering is an old custom south of the Yangzi. It seems to go back a long way, and was at its height in the Six Dynasties, as one can roughly tell from the poems. The lotus gatherers were young girls, who went out rowing little boats and singing love songs. Besides the gatherers, who of course were many, there were those who came to watch them. It was a lively season, and a romantic one. Emperor Yuan of Liang put it well in his *Rhapsody on Gathering Lotus*:

Then the handsome boys and lovely girls, rowing their boats, give their hearts; the bird-headed prows turn slowly, and they pass the winged cups to one another. As the oars are about to move, the weeds catch on them; as the boats begin to stir, the duckweed parts. Their slim waists bound in white silk, they linger and look back as they go. It is the start of summer and the end of spring, the leaves are tender and the flowers new; afraid of wetting their clothes they smile softly, afraid of tipping the boat they gather in their skirts.

From this one can see how they made merry in those days. It must have been delightful; what a pity we no longer have the luck to enjoy it.

Then I remembered lines from the *Song of Xizhou*:

Gathering lotus in the southern pond in autumn, the lotus flowers rise above our heads; I bend my head to play with the lotus seeds, and the lotus seeds are as clear as water.

If there were lotus gatherers here tonight, these lotus flowers too could be said to rise "above their heads." Only, there was no sign of flowing water, and that would never do. This made me long for the south after all. Thinking this, I looked up suddenly, and found I was already at my own door. Gently I pushed it open and went in. There was not a sound. My wife had long been fast asleep.

July 1927, Tsinghua Garden, Beijing.
