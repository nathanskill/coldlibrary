Behind our house there was a very large garden, known in the family as the Hundred-Plant Garden. It has long since been sold, together with the house, to the descendants of Zhu Xi, and it is seven or eight years now since I saw it for the last time. I believe there was really nothing in it but weeds; but in those days it was my paradise.

Never mind the green vegetable beds, the smooth stone railing round the well, the tall honey locust tree, the purple mulberries; never mind the cicadas droning in the leaves, the fat wasps resting on the cabbage flowers, the nimble lark suddenly shooting up from the grass into the clouds. The short mud wall round the garden alone was a source of endless delight. The crickets sang there softly, and the field crickets played their lutes. If you turned over a broken brick you might find a centipede, and there were blister beetles too: if you pressed a finger on one's back, it would go pop and let off a puff of smoke from behind. The vines of the fleeceflower and the climbing fig twined together; the fig bore fruit like lotus seed-pods, and the fleeceflower had swollen roots. Some people said that fleeceflower roots were shaped like people, and that if you ate one you would become an immortal. So I often pulled them up, pulling and pulling as they came out one after another, and I damaged the mud wall that way too, but I never found a root shaped like a person. If you did not mind the thorns, you could also pick raspberries, little balls like clusters of tiny coral beads, sour and sweet, far better than mulberries in both colour and flavour.

We did not go into the long grass, because it was said that a huge red-banded snake lived in the garden.

Mother Chang once told me a story. Long ago a scholar was studying in an old temple. One evening, as he was enjoying the cool in the courtyard, he suddenly heard someone calling his name. He answered, and looking round, saw a beautiful woman's face above the wall. She smiled at him and disappeared. He was delighted, but the old monk who came to chat with him that night saw through it. He said there was an evil look on the scholar's face; he must have met a Beauty Snake. This was a monster with a human head and a snake's body, which could call people by their names, and if anyone answered, it would come in the night to eat his flesh. Naturally the scholar was frightened to death, but the old monk told him not to worry, and gave him a little box, saying that if he put it beside his pillow he could sleep in peace. He did as he was told, but he could not sleep, and of course he could not. At midnight, sure enough, it came. Sha, sha, sha! Outside the door there was a sound like wind and rain. Just as he lay trembling all over, there was a whoosh, and a beam of golden light flew out from beside the pillow, and outside there was no sound at all. Then the golden light flew back and was gathered into the box. And then? Then the old monk said that it was a flying centipede, which could suck out a snake's brains, and the Beauty Snake had been killed by it.

The moral was this: if a strange voice calls your name, on no account answer it.

This story made me feel how dangerous life was. On summer evenings, as I sat out in the cool, I often felt uneasy and did not dare to look at the top of the wall, and I longed to have a box of flying centipedes like the old monk's. When I walked near the long grass in the Hundred-Plant Garden I often thought of it too. But to this day I have never got one, though I have never met the red-banded snake or the Beauty Snake either. Strange voices calling my name have of course been common enough, but none of them has been a Beauty Snake.

In winter the garden was rather dull; but once it snowed, things were different. Making snow prints (pressing your whole body into the snow) and building snow arhats needed people to admire them, and this was a deserted garden where hardly anyone came, so it was no place for that; we could only catch birds. A thin snow was no good. The snow had to cover the ground for a day or two, until the birds had gone hungry for a long time. You swept a patch of ground clear, propped up a big bamboo sieve on a short stick, scattered some husks of grain underneath, and tied a long string to the stick. Then you held the string from far off, watched the birds come down to peck, and when they were under the sieve, you gave the string a tug and they were trapped. Most of what we caught were sparrows, but there were white-cheeked "Zhang Fei birds" too, so fierce-tempered that they would not live through the night in a cage.

It was Runtu's father who taught me this way, but I was not much good at it. I would clearly see the birds go in, pull the string, run over to look, and find nothing there; after half a day's work I might catch three or four. Runtu's father could catch dozens in a few hours, and stuff them into his bag, where they would chirp and bump about. I once asked him the secret of his success. He only smiled quietly and said, "You're too impatient. You don't wait for them to get into the middle."

I do not know why my family decided to send me to school, and to the school with the strictest reputation in the whole town, at that. Perhaps it was because I had damaged the mud wall pulling up fleeceflower roots, perhaps because I had thrown bricks over into the Liangs' garden next door, perhaps because I had jumped down from the stone railing round the well ... there is no way of knowing. In any case, I would no longer be able to go often to the Hundred-Plant Garden. Ade, my crickets! Ade, my raspberries and climbing figs!

Less than half a li east of our gate, over a stone bridge, was my teacher's house. You went in through a black-lacquered bamboo gate, and the third room was the schoolroom. In the middle of the wall hung a board inscribed "Three-Flavour Study," and under it a painting of a very fat spotted deer lying under an old tree. There was no tablet to Confucius, so we bowed before the board and the deer. The first bow was to Confucius, the second to the teacher.

When we made the second bow, the teacher returned it kindly from one side. He was a tall, thin old man with grizzled hair and beard, and he wore big spectacles. I had great respect for him, for I had long heard that he was the most upright, simple and learned man in the town.

I don't know where I had heard that Dongfang Shuo, who was also very learned, knew of an insect called "Strange-Indeed," which was formed from the grievance of the wronged, and which would melt away if you poured wine on it. I very much wanted to know this story in detail, but Ah Chang did not know it, for after all she was not learned. Now was my chance; I could ask the teacher.

"Sir, what is this insect called 'Strange-Indeed'? ..." I asked hurriedly, at the end of my lesson, just as I was about to leave.

"I don't know." He seemed very displeased, and there was even anger on his face.

Then I learned that a pupil should not ask about such things, but only study. For he was a learned scholar of the old school, and he could not possibly not know; when he said he did not know, it meant he did not want to say. People older than I am have often been like that; I have met with it many times.

So I only studied. At noon I practised writing characters, and in the evening I practised matching couplets. For the first few days the teacher was very strict with me, but later he became kinder. Still, the lessons he gave me to read grew longer and longer, and the couplets I had to match grew longer by a character at a time, from three characters to five, and at last to seven.

Behind the Three-Flavour Study there was also a garden. It was small, but there too you could climb up on the flower terrace to pick wintersweet, or look for the cast skins of cicadas on the ground or in the osmanthus tree. The best thing to do was to catch flies and feed them to the ants, which made no noise at all. But if too many of us went into the garden, or stayed out too long, it would not do, and the teacher would shout from the schoolroom:

"Where has everyone gone?"

Then we would go back one by one; going back all together would not do either. He had a ferule, but he seldom used it; there was also the rule of kneeling as a punishment, but he seldom used that either. As a rule he only glared at us for a while and shouted:

"Read!"

Then we would all read aloud at the top of our voices, a real hubbub. Some read "Is goodness far away? If I want goodness, goodness comes," some read "To laugh at someone who has lost his teeth, say 'The dog's door is wide open,'" some read "Nine at the top: the hidden dragon, do not act," and some read "Its soil is of the lower-upper grade, its tribute of the upper-mixed; its tribute is wrapped rushes, oranges and pomelos" ... The teacher read aloud too. After a while our voices dropped and fell silent, and only he went on reading loudly:

"The iron sceptre, waved with grace, astonishes all the company, ah ...; the golden goblet, overturned and dripping, a thousand cups and still not drunk, oh ..."

I suspected this must be very fine writing, because whenever he reached this passage he would smile, tilt his head back, and sway it, bending further and further backwards.

When the teacher was absorbed in his reading, it suited us very well. Some of us would put paper helmets on our fingernails and stage plays. I drew pictures, using a kind of paper called "Jingchuan paper," laid over the illustrations in novels to trace the figures one by one, the way we traced characters when practising writing. The more books I read, the more pictures I drew; I never became much of a scholar, but I had quite a collection of drawings. The most complete were the illustrations to *Quelling the Bandits* and *Journey to the West*, a big volume of each. Later, needing money, I sold them to a rich classmate. His father kept a shop selling tinfoil paper for offerings; I hear that he is now the master of the shop himself, and will soon rise to the rank of the gentry. Those drawings must have vanished long ago.

(18 September)
