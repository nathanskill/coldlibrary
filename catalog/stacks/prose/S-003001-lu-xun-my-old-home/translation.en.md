Braving the bitter cold, I travelled more than two thousand li back to the old home I had left over twenty years before.

It was the depth of winter. As we drew near, the sky clouded over, and a cold wind whistled into the cabin of the boat. Looking out through a crack in the awning, I saw a few desolate villages lying scattered, near and far, under a sallow sky, without the least sign of life. My heart sank in spite of me.

Ah. Was this really the home I had kept in my memory for twenty years?

The home I remembered was not like this at all. My home was much better. Yet if I tried to recall its beauty and name its good points, I had no image of them, no words for them. Perhaps it had always been like this. So I explained it to myself: home had always been this way. It had not improved, but it was not as dreary as I felt it to be; only my mood had changed, because this time I had not come home in a cheerful frame of mind.

I had come this time solely to bid it farewell. The old house in which our clan had lived together for so many years had been sold to another family, and it had to be handed over before the end of the year. So we had to leave before New Year's Day, saying goodbye forever to the familiar old house and the familiar old place, and move to the strange town where I now earned my living.

Early the next morning I reached the gate of our house. Broken stems of withered grass on the roof tiles trembled in the wind, explaining well enough why this old house was bound to change owners. Several branches of the family seemed to have moved out already, so it was very quiet. When I reached our own rooms, my mother was already coming out to meet me, and my eight-year-old nephew Hong'er came rushing out after her.

My mother was very pleased, though she was also hiding a good deal of sadness. She told me to sit down, rest and drink some tea, and said nothing about moving for the time being. Hong'er, who had never seen me before, stood at a distance, just staring.

But at last we did talk about the move. I said that I had already rented lodgings in the other town and bought some furniture, and that the rest would have to be bought after we sold all the wooden things here. My mother agreed. She said the luggage was nearly all packed, and about half the furniture that was too awkward to take had already been sold, only she had not been able to collect the money.

"Rest for a day or two, and go round once to call on our relatives, and then we can leave," my mother said.

"Yes."

"And there is Runtu. Every time he comes here he asks after you, and he wants very much to see you once more. I have told him roughly when you would be back, so he may come any time."

At that moment a strange and wonderful picture flashed into my mind: a round golden moon hanging in a deep blue sky, and below it a stretch of sandy ground by the sea, planted as far as the eye could see with jade-green watermelons. In the middle of them stood a boy of eleven or twelve, a silver ring around his neck and a steel pitchfork in his hand, thrusting with all his might at a zha, and the zha twisted its body and slipped away between his legs.

That boy was Runtu. When I first knew him I was just over ten myself, so it was nearly thirty years ago. My father was still alive then, the family was well off, and I was a young master. That year it was our family's turn to take charge of a great ancestral sacrifice. The duty was said to come round only once in thirty years or more, so it was taken very seriously. In the first month the ancestral portraits were set out, the offerings were many, the ritual vessels were very fine, a great many people came to worship, and the vessels had to be guarded against thieves. We had only one busy-month man. (In our part of the country there are three kinds of hired hands: those who work all year for one family are called year-men; those hired by the day are called day-men; and those who farm their own land but come to work for a particular family only at New Year, at festivals, and when rents are collected are called busy-month men.) He could not manage everything alone, so he told my father he could send for his son Runtu to look after the sacrificial vessels.

My father agreed, and I was delighted, for I had long heard the name Runtu and knew that he was about my own age. He had been born in an intercalary month, and since the five elements of his horoscope lacked earth, his father named him Runtu, "Intercalary Earth." He could set traps and catch small birds.

So I looked forward to New Year every day, for New Year would bring Runtu. At last the end of the year came, and one day my mother told me that Runtu had arrived, and I flew to see him. He was in the kitchen: a round, ruddy-purple face, a little felt cap on his head, and a gleaming silver ring around his neck, which showed how much his father loved him; he had been afraid the boy might die, so he had made a vow before the gods and the Buddha and used the ring to hold him fast. Runtu was very shy with people, but not with me. When no one else was about he would talk to me, and in less than half a day we were friends.

I don't know what we talked about then. I only remember that Runtu was very happy, and said that since coming to town he had seen a great many things he had never seen before.

The next day I wanted him to catch birds. He said:

"Can't be done. We have to wait for a heavy snow. On our sandy land, after it snows, I sweep a clear patch, prop up a big bamboo sieve with a short stick, scatter husks underneath, and when the birds come to eat, I give one pull from far off on the string tied to the stick, and the birds are caught under the sieve. All kinds: rice-birds, horned pheasants, wood pigeons, blue-backs ..."

So then I longed for snow as well.

Runtu also said to me:

"It's too cold now. You come to us in summer. In the daytime we go to the seashore to pick up shells, red ones and green ones, and ghost-fright shells and Guanyin's-hand shells too. At night I go with my dad to guard the watermelons. You can come too."

"Guard them against thieves?"

"No. If someone walking by is thirsty and picks a melon to eat, folks round us don't count that as stealing. What we guard against are badgers, hedgehogs and zha. In the moonlight, you listen, and there's a crunching sound: that's a zha biting a melon. Then you take your pitchfork and creep up quietly ..."

I had no idea then what this thing called a zha was, and I don't know even now. For no particular reason I imagined it as something like a small dog, and very fierce.

"Doesn't it bite people?"

"You've got the pitchfork. You creep up, you see the zha, and you stab. The creature is very clever. It runs straight at you and gets away between your legs. Its fur is as slippery as oil ..."

I had never known that the world was full of so many fresh things: that the seashore had shells of every colour like that, and that watermelons led such dangerous lives. Before, I had only known them as things sold in the fruit shop.

"On our sand, when the tide is coming in, there are lots of jumping fish that just keep jumping. They have two legs like a frog's ..."

Ah. Runtu's mind was full of endless marvels, none of which my usual friends knew about. They knew nothing of these things. While Runtu was by the sea, they, like me, saw only the square of sky above the high walls of the courtyard.

Unfortunately the first month came to an end and Runtu had to go home. I cried bitterly, and he hid in the kitchen, crying and refusing to come out, but in the end his father took him away. Later he sent me, through his father, a packet of shells and a few very pretty feathers, and I sent him things once or twice, but we never saw each other again.

Now that my mother had mentioned him, these childhood memories came back to life all at once, like lightning, and I seemed to glimpse my beautiful old home. I answered:

"That's wonderful. And how is he ...?"

"He? ... Things haven't gone well for him either ..." As she spoke, my mother looked out of the room. "Those people are here again. They say they've come to buy furniture, and they help themselves to whatever they like on the way. I must go and see."

My mother got up and went out. Several women's voices could be heard outside the door. I called Hong'er over and chatted with him: I asked whether he could write, and whether he would like to go away.

"Shall we go by train?"

"Yes, we shall go by train."

"And by boat?"

"By boat first ..."

"Ha. So this is how he looks now. Such a long moustache." A strange, shrill voice suddenly cried out.

I gave a start and looked up quickly. In front of me stood a woman of about fifty, with prominent cheekbones and thin lips. She had her hands on her hips, wore no skirt over her trousers, and stood with her legs apart, just like the compass in a box of drawing instruments, with its thin, spindly legs.

I was taken aback.

"Don't you know me? I used to carry you in my arms."

I was even more taken aback. Fortunately my mother came in just then and said from the side:

"He's been away so many years, he has forgotten everything. You ought to remember," she said, turning to me. "This is Mrs Yang, the second sister-in-law from across the road ... who keeps the beancurd shop."

Oh, I remembered. When I was a child there had indeed been a Mrs Yang who sat all day in the beancurd shop across the road, and people called her the Beauty of the Beancurd Shop. But she used to powder her face, her cheekbones were not so high then, nor her lips so thin. And since she sat all day, I had never seen her in this compass-like pose. People said at the time that it was thanks to her that the beancurd shop did such good business. But probably because of my age, she had made no impression on me whatever, and so I had forgotten her entirely. The compass, however, was most indignant. She looked at me with contempt, as if laughing at a Frenchman who had never heard of Napoleon or an American who had never heard of Washington, and said with a cold smile:

"Forgotten? Well, the great look down on the rest of us ..."

"Not at all ... I ..." I stood up, flustered.

"Then let me tell you something, Brother Xun. You've grown rich, and these things are heavy to move. What do you want with this broken old furniture? Let me take it. Small folk like us can use it."

"I haven't grown rich at all. I have to sell these things to buy ..."

"Oh dear, oh dear, you've been made an intendant and you still say you're not rich? You have three concubines now, and when you go out it's in a great sedan chair with eight bearers, and you still say you're not rich? Hah, you can't hide anything from me."

I knew there was nothing more to be said, so I closed my mouth and stood in silence.

"Oh dear, oh dear, it's true: the more money they have, the less they'll part with a hair of it, and the less they'll part with a hair of it, the more money they have ..." Muttering on and on, the compass turned away indignantly and walked slowly out, slipping a pair of my mother's gloves into the waist of her trousers as she went.

After this, relatives and members of the clan who lived nearby came to call. I received them, and whenever I had a moment I packed a few things. Three or four days passed like this.

One very cold afternoon, after lunch, I was sitting drinking tea when I sensed someone coming in, and turned to look. When I saw who it was I was greatly startled, jumped up, and hurried to meet him.

The newcomer was Runtu. Although I knew him at first glance, he was no longer the Runtu of my memory. He was twice the size he had been. His round, ruddy face had turned a greyish yellow and was lined with deep wrinkles. His eyes, like his father's, were swollen and red all round; I knew that this is how most people who farm by the sea become, exposed to the sea wind all day. On his head was a shabby felt cap, and he wore only a very thin padded jacket, so that he was shivering all over. In his hand he carried a paper package and a long pipe, and the hand was not the plump, rosy hand I remembered, but coarse and clumsy and cracked, like the bark of a pine tree.

I was very excited, but I did not know what to say, and could only say:

"Ah, Brother Runtu, so you've come ...?"

Then a great many things came to me that I wanted to pour out one after another like a string of beads: horned pheasants, jumping fish, shells, zha ... But something seemed to hold them back. They only went round and round in my head and would not come out of my mouth.

He stood still, and a look of both joy and sadness came over his face. His lips moved, but he made no sound. At last his manner became respectful, and he said clearly:

"Master ...!"

I felt a shiver run through me. I knew then that a lamentable thick wall had grown up between us. I could not say anything either.

He turned his head and said, "Shuisheng, kowtow to the master." And he pulled forward a child who had been hiding behind his back. This was just the Runtu of twenty years before, only paler and thinner, and with no silver ring around his neck. "This is my fifth child. He hasn't seen the world, so he hangs back ..."

My mother and Hong'er came downstairs. They had probably heard our voices.

"Madam, I got your letter long ago. I was so glad to hear the master was coming home ..." said Runtu.

"Oh, why are you so formal? Didn't you two call each other brother in the old days? Just call him Brother Xun as you used to," said my mother cheerfully.

"Oh, madam, really ... What would that look like? We were children then and didn't know any better ..." As he spoke, Runtu called Shuisheng forward again to bow, but the child was shy and stayed pressed close behind his father's back.

"So this is Shuisheng? The fifth? Everyone here is a stranger to him, no wonder he's shy. Let Hong'er take him out for a walk," said my mother.

When Hong'er heard this he went over to Shuisheng, and Shuisheng went out with him quite easily. My mother asked Runtu to sit down. He hesitated for a moment, then at last took a seat, leaned his long pipe against the table, and handed over the paper package, saying:

"There's nothing much in winter. These few dried green beans we dried ourselves. Please, master ..."

I asked him how things were with him. He only shook his head.

"Very hard. Even my sixth child can help now, but there's never enough to eat ... and no peace either ... Money wanted everywhere, and no fixed rules about it ... and the harvests are bad. You grow things and carry them to market, and you have to pay several taxes on the way and lose money. If you don't sell them, they just rot ..."

He only shook his head. His face was carved with many wrinkles, yet it did not move at all, like a stone statue. Probably he felt only the bitterness and could not put it into words. After a moment's silence he took up his pipe and smoked without a word.

My mother questioned him and learned that he was very busy at home and would have to go back the next day. He had not had lunch, so she told him to go to the kitchen and fry some rice for himself.

He went out, and my mother and I sighed over his circumstances: too many children, famine, heavy taxes, soldiers, bandits, officials, gentry, all of them had ground him down until he was like a wooden puppet. My mother told me that whatever we did not need to take with us we could give to him, and let him choose for himself.

In the afternoon he picked out a few things: two long tables, four chairs, an incense burner and candlesticks, and a steelyard. He also asked for all the ashes from the stove (in our part of the country we burn rice straw for cooking, and the ashes can be used to fertilize sandy soil), and said he would come for them with a boat when we left.

That night we talked again about this and that, nothing of importance. The next morning he went home with Shuisheng.

Nine days later came the day of our departure. Runtu arrived in the morning. Shuisheng had not come with him; he had brought only a five-year-old daughter to watch the boat. We were busy all day and had no more time to talk. There were a good many visitors too, some to see us off, some to take things away, and some to see us off and take things away. By the time we went aboard the boat at dusk, every old and broken thing in the house, large and small, coarse and fine, had been swept away.

Our boat moved forward. The green hills on both banks had taken on a deep blue-black in the twilight, and receded one after another past the stern.

Hong'er and I leaned against the cabin window, looking out together at the blurred scenery, when he suddenly asked:

"Uncle, when are we coming back?"

"Coming back? Why are you thinking of coming back before you've even left?"

"But Shuisheng has asked me to go to his home and play ..." His big black eyes were wide open as he thought about it.

My mother and I both felt a little lost, and so we talked about Runtu again. My mother said that Mrs Yang, the Beauty of the Beancurd Shop, had come every day since we began packing, and the day before yesterday she had dug a dozen or so bowls and dishes out of the ash heap. After some discussion she had decided that Runtu had buried them there so that he could carry them home along with the ashes. Mrs Yang, feeling she had done something very clever, took the dog-vexer (a contraption we use for feeding chickens in our part of the country: a wooden tray with a railing over it holding the feed; the chickens can stretch their necks in to peck, but a dog cannot, and can only look at it and fume) and ran off as if she were flying. It was remarkable that she could run so fast on those little bound feet in their high-soled shoes.

The old house was left further and further behind, and the hills and rivers of home were gradually left behind too, but I felt no great regret. I only felt that all around me was an invisible high wall, cutting me off and leaving me alone, and it oppressed me terribly. The image of that little hero with the silver ring in the watermelon field, once so clear to me, had suddenly become blurred, and this made me terribly sad as well.

My mother and Hong'er were both asleep.

I lay there, listening to the water rippling under the bottom of the boat, and knew that I was going my own way. I thought: I am cut off from Runtu so completely now, yet the young ones are still of one mind; isn't Hong'er thinking of Shuisheng at this very moment? I hoped they would not be like us, would not grow apart from each other in their turn ... But I did not want them, for the sake of being of one mind, to live the toilsome, drifting life I have lived; nor did I want them to live the toilsome, numbed life Runtu has lived; nor the toilsome, reckless life that others live. They should have a new life, one that we have never lived.

When I thought of hope, I was suddenly afraid. When Runtu asked for the incense burner and candlesticks, I had laughed at him secretly, thinking he was still worshipping idols and could never put them out of his mind. Yet what I called hope, was it not an idol of my own making? Only his wish was close at hand, and mine far off and hazy.

As I dozed, a stretch of jade-green sand by the sea spread out before my eyes, and above it a round golden moon hung in a deep blue sky. I thought: hope cannot be said to exist, nor can it be said not to exist. It is just like the roads across the earth. For in fact the earth had no roads to begin with, but when many people pass one way, a road is made.

(January 1921)
