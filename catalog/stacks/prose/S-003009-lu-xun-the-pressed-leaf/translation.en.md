Reading the *Yanmen Ji* by lamplight, I suddenly came upon a pressed maple leaf.

It reminded me of late autumn last year. Heavy frost fell at night, most of the leaves on the trees withered and dropped, and the little maple in front of the courtyard turned red too. I walked round and round it, looking closely at the colours of its leaves, which I had never noticed so carefully when they were green. Not the whole tree was red. Most of the leaves were a pale crimson, and a few, on a ground of scarlet, still had patches of deep green. One leaf had a single wormhole edged with black, and among the mottling of red, yellow and green it gazed out at you like a bright eye. I thought to myself: this is a sick leaf. So I picked it and slipped it into the *Yanmen Ji* I had just bought. I suppose I wanted to keep, for a while, these mottled colours of a leaf that had been eaten and was about to fall, so that it would not be scattered at once with all the others.

But tonight it lies before me like yellow wax, and its eye no longer shines as it did last year. In a few more years, when its old colours have faded from my memory, I am afraid even I will not know why it was put in the book. The mottled beauty of a sick leaf about to fall, it seems, can only be faced for the briefest time, let alone the lush green of a living one. Looking out of the window, I see that even the hardiest trees have long been stripped bare, to say nothing of the maple. In late autumn there may well have been sick leaves like last year's one, but unfortunately this year I had no leisure to enjoy the autumn trees.

(26 December 1925)
