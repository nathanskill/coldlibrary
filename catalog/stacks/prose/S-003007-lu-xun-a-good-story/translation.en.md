The lamp flame is slowly shrinking, a warning that the paraffin is nearly gone; and the paraffin is not a good brand, so the chimney has long been blackened with smoke. Firecrackers are crackling all around, and tobacco smoke hangs about me: it is a dim, heavy night.

I close my eyes and lean back in my chair. My hand, holding the *Chuxue Ji*, rests on my knee.

In my drowsiness I see a good story.

It is a very beautiful story, graceful and full of charm. Many beautiful people and beautiful things are interwoven like a sky of brocade clouds, flying like ten thousand shooting stars, and at the same time spreading out without end.

I seem to remember once passing along the Shanyin road in a little boat. On both banks the tallow trees, the young rice, the wild flowers, the chickens and dogs, the thickets and the bare trees, the thatched cottages, a pagoda, a temple, farmers and village women, village girls, clothes hung out to dry, monks, rain capes, sky, clouds, bamboos ... were all reflected in the clear green stream, and with every stroke of the oar they each caught the sparkle of the sunlight, and swayed together with the duckweed and the fish in the water. All the reflections and all the things broke apart and wavered, spread wider and blended with one another; and no sooner had they blended than they drew back and almost returned to their former shapes. Their edges were ragged like summer clouds, lined with sunlight, giving off a mercury-coloured flame. Every river I have travelled has been like this.

The story I see now is like this too. Against the blue sky in the water, everything is interwoven upon it, woven into one piece, always alive, always unfolding, and I cannot see the end of it.

The few spindly hollyhocks under the withered willows by the river must have been planted by the village girls. Big red flowers and mottled red flowers all float and stir in the water, suddenly breaking up and stretching out into threads of rouge-coloured water, yet with no blur. The cottages, the dogs, the pagoda, the village girls, the clouds ... are all floating and stirring too. The big red flowers, one after another, are drawn out into a dashing, leaping ribbon of red brocade. The ribbon is woven into the dog, the dog into the white cloud, the white cloud into the village girl ... In a moment they will draw back again. But the reflections of the mottled red flowers have already broken up and stretched out, and are about to weave themselves into the pagoda, the village girl, the dog, the cottage, the clouds.

Now the story I see grows clearer, beautiful, graceful, full of charm, and bright. Above the blue sky there are countless beautiful people and beautiful stories. I see each one, I know each one.

I am about to gaze at them ...

Just as I am about to gaze at them, I start, and open my eyes. The brocade clouds have crumpled and become confused, as though someone had flung a big stone into the river, so that the waves suddenly rise and tear the whole picture to shreds. Without thinking I hastily catch the *Chuxue Ji*, which has almost slipped to the floor, and before my eyes there are still a few rainbow-coloured fragments.

I truly love this good story. While the fragments are still there, I want to bring it back, finish it, keep it. I throw down the book, lean forward and reach for a pen. But where is there a single fragment? I see only the dim lamplight. I am not in the little boat.

But I will always remember having seen this good story, on a dim, heavy night ...

(24 February 1925)
