From my back garden two trees can be seen beyond the wall. One is a date tree, and the other is also a date tree.

The night sky above them is strange and high. I have never in my life seen so strange and high a sky. It seems to want to leave the human world, so that people looking up will no longer see it. Yet now it is extremely blue, blinking with scores of starry eyes, cold eyes. A faint smile shows at the corners of its mouth, as if it thought itself very profound, and it scatters heavy frost on the wild flowers and grasses in my garden.

I do not know what those flowers and grasses are really called, or what people call them. I remember one that bore tiny pink flowers. It is still blooming, but its flowers are even tinier now. Shivering in the cold night air, she is dreaming. She dreams of the coming of spring, dreams of the coming of autumn, dreams of a thin poet wiping his tears on her last petal and telling her that though autumn comes and winter comes, spring will follow after, when butterflies flutter everywhere and the bees all sing their songs of spring. Then she smiles, though her colour has turned a pitiful red with the cold, and she goes on shivering.

As for the date trees, they have shed all their leaves. Before, there were still one or two children who came to knock down the dates others had missed, but now not one is left, and the leaves are all gone too. The date tree knows the little pink flower's dream, that after autumn comes spring; and he knows too the dream of the fallen leaves, that after spring there is still autumn. He has shed every leaf and kept only his trunk and branches; but, freed from the curve of the days when he was laden with fruit and leaves, he stretches himself very comfortably. A few of his boughs, though, still hang low, nursing the wounds the poles gave them when the dates were knocked down; while the straightest and longest of his boughs pierce silently, like iron, into the strange and high sky, so that the sky winks its sly eyes; they pierce the full round moon in the sky, so that the moon turns pale with embarrassment.

The slyly winking sky grows bluer and bluer, more and more uneasy, as if it wanted to leave the human world and escape the date tree, leaving only the moon behind. But the moon too has hidden herself quietly in the east, while the bare trunk still pierces silently, like iron, into the strange and high sky, bent on dealing it a mortal blow, no matter how many bewitching eyes it blinks at him.

With a shriek, an evil bird flies past on its night prowl.

Suddenly I hear a midnight laugh, a low chuckle, as if not wanting to wake the sleepers, yet all the air around echoes with the laughter. It is midnight, and there is no one else about. At once I know that the sound is coming from my own mouth, and at once I am driven by this laughter back into my room. At once I turn up the wick of my lamp.

The glass of the back window rattles, as a swarm of small insects keeps butting against it. Before long a few come in, perhaps through a hole in the window paper. Once inside, they rattle against the glass chimney of the lamp. One of them plunges in from the top and meets the flame, and I believe the flame is real. Two or three others rest on the paper lampshade, panting. That shade was put on new last night: snow-white paper folded in wavy creases, with a sprig of scarlet gardenia painted in one corner.

When the scarlet gardenia blooms, the date tree will dream the little pink flower's dream again, and curve again, green and luxuriant ... I hear the midnight laugh again. I hastily cut short my train of thought and look at the little green insects that are still on the white paper. Big-headed and small-tailed, like sunflower seeds, they are only half the size of a grain of wheat, and their whole bodies are a lovely, pitiable green.

I give a yawn, light a cigarette, puff out the smoke, and in silence, before the lamp, I pay my respects to these delicate green heroes.

(15 September 1924)
