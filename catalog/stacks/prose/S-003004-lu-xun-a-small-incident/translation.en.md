It is six years now, in the twinkling of an eye, since I came from the country to the capital. In that time I have heard and seen a good many so-called affairs of state, but none of them has left any trace in my heart. If I tried to name their effect on me, it would only be that they made my temper worse; to be honest, they taught me to think less of people day by day.

But one small incident has had a meaning for me. It dragged me away from my bad temper, and to this day I cannot forget it.

It was the winter of the sixth year of the Republic. A strong north wind was blowing hard, and to earn my living I had to be out on the road early in the morning. There was hardly anyone about, and with great difficulty I managed to hire a rickshaw to take me to S Gate. After a while the north wind dropped a little. By now the dust had been blown clean off the road, leaving a clean, white highway, and the rickshaw man ran faster. Just as we were nearing S Gate, someone was caught on the shafts of the rickshaw and slowly fell to the ground.

It was a woman, with greying hair and very ragged clothes. She had suddenly cut across in front of us from the side of the road. The rickshaw man had swerved to make way, but her tattered padded waistcoat was unbuttoned and, flapping open in the breeze, it caught on the shaft. Luckily the rickshaw man had already slowed down, or she would have taken a bad fall and cut her head open.

She lay on the ground, and the rickshaw man stopped too. I was sure the old woman was not hurt, and no one else had seen it, so I was annoyed with him for meddling, for making trouble for himself and holding me up.

So I said to him, "It's nothing. Go on."

The rickshaw man paid no attention (or perhaps he did not hear me). He put down the shafts, helped the old woman slowly to her feet, held her by the arm to steady her, and said to her:

"What's the matter?"

"I'm hurt."

I thought: I saw you fall slowly to the ground. How could you be hurt? This is only play-acting. It's really hateful. The rickshaw man was asking for trouble by meddling; now let him see how he gets out of it.

But when the rickshaw man heard what the old woman said, he did not hesitate for a moment. Still holding her arm, he walked forward step by step. I was rather surprised, and looked ahead quickly: there was a police station there. After the high wind there was nobody outside. The rickshaw man was helping the old woman straight towards its gate.

At that moment I suddenly had a strange feeling. His dusty figure, seen from behind, seemed in an instant to grow larger, and the further he walked the larger he grew, until I had to look up to see him. And for me he gradually became almost a kind of pressure, which even seemed about to squeeze out the "smallness" hidden under my fur-lined gown.

My vitality seemed somehow to have frozen. I sat there without moving and without thinking, until I saw a policeman come out of the station, and then I got down from the rickshaw.

The policeman came up to me and said, "Hire another rickshaw. He can't pull you any more."

Without thinking, I pulled a handful of coppers out of my coat pocket and handed them to the policeman. "Please give him these ..."

The wind had dropped completely, and the street was still quiet. As I walked along, I thought, but I almost dared not think about myself. Leaving aside what had happened before, what was the meaning of that handful of coppers? A reward for him? Was I fit to judge the rickshaw man? I could not answer myself.

To this day I still often remember that incident. Because of it I often bear the pain of trying hard to think about myself. The civil rule and military might of these years have long been to me like the "Confucius said" and "the Book of Songs says" I recited as a small boy: I could not repeat half a line of them. Only this small incident keeps floating before my eyes, sometimes even more clearly than before, making me ashamed, urging me to renew myself, and adding to my courage and my hope.

(July 1920)
