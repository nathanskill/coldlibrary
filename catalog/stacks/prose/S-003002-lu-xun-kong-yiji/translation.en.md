The wine shops of Luzhen are laid out differently from those elsewhere. Each has a big counter shaped like a carpenter's square, facing the street, with hot water kept ready inside it so that wine can be warmed at any moment. At noon or in the evening, when work is over, labourers will often spend four coppers on a bowl of wine (that was over twenty years ago; a bowl now costs ten), and drink it hot, standing outside the counter, to rest. If they are willing to spend one copper more, they can buy a dish of salted bamboo shoots or aniseed beans to go with the wine, and for a dozen or so coppers they could buy a meat dish. But most of these customers belonged to the short-jacket crowd, and few of them could afford such luxury. Only those in long gowns would stroll into the room next to the shop, order wine and food, and sit drinking at their leisure.

From the age of twelve I worked as a boy in the Prosperity Tavern at the edge of the town. The tavern keeper said I looked too foolish to wait on the long-gowned customers, so I was given work in the front of the shop. Though the short-jacket customers there were easier to talk to, there were quite a few among them who grumbled and nagged without end. They liked to watch with their own eyes as the yellow wine was ladled out of the jar, to see whether there was water at the bottom of the wine pot, and to watch the pot being set in the hot water before they would rest easy. Under such strict supervision it was hard to slip water into the wine. So after a few days the tavern keeper said I was not up to this job either. Luckily the man who had recommended me carried some weight, so I could not be dismissed, and I was moved to the dull work of warming wine and nothing else.

From then on I stood all day behind the counter, attending to my one duty. Although I never failed in it, I found it monotonous and dull. The tavern keeper had a fierce face, and the customers were in no better temper, so there was no chance to be lively. Only when Kong Yiji came to the tavern could I laugh a little, and that is why I still remember him.

Kong Yiji was the only long-gowned customer who drank his wine standing. He was a big man, with a pale, greenish face and scars often showing among his wrinkles, and a straggling grizzled beard. He wore a long gown, but it was dirty and torn and looked as if it had not been mended or washed for ten years or more. When he talked to people his speech was always stuffed with archaic particles, so that you could only half understand him. Because his surname was Kong, people gave him the nickname Kong Yiji, from the half-intelligible phrase in children's copybooks, "Shang da ren Kong Yi ji." Whenever Kong Yiji came into the tavern, all the drinkers would look at him and laugh, and someone would call out:

"Kong Yiji, you've got some new scars on your face."

He would not answer, but would say to the counter, "Warm two bowls of wine, and a dish of aniseed beans," and lay out nine coppers. Then they would shout on purpose: "You must have been stealing again."

Kong Yiji would open his eyes wide and say, "How can you ruin a man's good name without any grounds ..."

"Good name? The day before yesterday I saw you with my own eyes, strung up and beaten for stealing books from the He family."

Then Kong Yiji would flush, the veins on his forehead would stand out, and he would protest, "Taking books cannot be counted as stealing ... Taking books ... the affairs of a scholar, can that be called stealing?" Then followed quotations hard to understand, such as "A gentleman keeps firm in poverty," and a string of archaic particles, which made everybody roar with laughter, and the whole tavern, inside and out, was filled with cheerful air.

I heard people say behind his back that Kong Yiji had studied the classics but had never passed the official examination, and had no way of making a living, so that he grew poorer and poorer until he was nearly reduced to begging. Luckily he wrote a good hand, and could copy books for people in exchange for a bowl of rice. Unfortunately he had a bad failing too: he loved drinking and hated work. After a few days he would disappear, together with the books, paper, brushes and inkstone. After this had happened several times, nobody wanted him to copy books any more. Then Kong Yiji had no way out but to do a little stealing now and then. But in our tavern his conduct was better than anyone else's: he never failed to pay. Although sometimes he had no ready money and his name would be chalked up on the board, in less than a month he would settle his debt without fail, and the name Kong Yiji would be wiped off the board.

When Kong Yiji had drunk half a bowl of wine, his flushed face would gradually return to normal, and someone standing by would ask him, "Kong Yiji, can you really read?"

Kong Yiji would look at the questioner with an air of disdaining such an argument. Then they would go on: "How is it you never managed to pass even the lowest examination?"

At once Kong Yiji would look dejected and ill at ease. His face would turn grey and his lips would move, but this time everything he said was archaic particles, and none of it could be understood at all. Then everybody would roar with laughter again, and the tavern, inside and out, would be filled with cheerful air.

At such times I could join in the laughter, and the tavern keeper would never scold me for it. In fact, whenever he saw Kong Yiji, the keeper himself would often ask him the same questions, to make people laugh. Kong Yiji knew that he could not talk with them, so he could only talk to children. Once he said to me:

"Have you had any schooling?"

I nodded slightly.

He said, "So you've had some schooling ... Then let me test you. How do you write the hui in hui-xiang beans?"

I thought: is a beggar like this fit to test me? I turned my face away and paid him no more attention. Kong Yiji waited a long time, then said very earnestly:

"You can't write it? ... I'll teach you. Mind you remember. These characters ought to be remembered. When you become a tavern keeper some day, you'll need them for your accounts."

It seemed to me I was still a long way from the rank of tavern keeper, and besides, our tavern keeper never put aniseed beans in the accounts. Half amused and half impatient, I answered him lazily:

"Who needs you to teach me? Isn't it the hui with the grass radical on top of the hui that means to return?"

Kong Yiji looked extremely pleased. Tapping two long fingernails on the counter, he nodded and said, "Right, right ... There are four ways of writing hui. Do you know them?"

I grew more impatient, pouted, and walked away. Kong Yiji had just dipped his fingernail in wine, ready to write the characters on the counter, but when he saw how little interest I had, he sighed again and looked extremely regretful.

Several times the neighbourhood children, hearing laughter, came to join in the fun and surrounded Kong Yiji. Then he would give them aniseed beans, one apiece. After eating the beans, the children would still hang about, their eyes on the dish. Flustered, Kong Yiji would cover it with his hand, bend down and say, "Not many left, I haven't many left." Then, straightening up, he would look at the beans again, shake his head and say, "Not many, not many. Many? Not many, truly." And the children would scatter in a burst of laughter.

That was how Kong Yiji made people happy, but without him, they got along just as well.

One day, perhaps two or three days before the Mid-Autumn Festival, the tavern keeper was slowly making up his accounts. He took down the board, and suddenly said, "Kong Yiji hasn't been in for a long time. He still owes nineteen coppers." Only then did I realize that he had indeed not been in for a long time.

One of the drinkers said, "How could he come? ... His legs have been broken."

"Oh," said the tavern keeper.

"He kept on stealing. This time he was fool enough to steal from the house of Mr Ding, the provincial graduate. As if anyone could steal from that family."

"What happened then?"

"What happened? First he had to write a confession, then he was beaten. The beating went on nearly all night, and then they broke his legs."

"And then?"

"And then his legs were broken."

"And after they were broken?"

"After? ... Who knows? Maybe he's dead."

The tavern keeper asked no more, but went on slowly making up his accounts.

After the Mid-Autumn Festival the wind grew colder day by day, and winter was drawing near. Even though I spent all day by the stove, I had to wear a padded jacket. One afternoon, when there was not a single customer, I was sitting with my eyes closed when suddenly I heard a voice:

"Warm a bowl of wine."

The voice was very low, but familiar. When I looked, there was no one there. I stood up and looked out, and there was Kong Yiji sitting on the ground below the counter, facing the threshold. His face was dark and thin, and he looked a wreck. He wore a ragged lined jacket and sat cross-legged on a straw mat, which was tied to his shoulders with a straw rope. When he saw me, he said again:

"Warm a bowl of wine."

The tavern keeper leaned out and said, "Is that Kong Yiji? You still owe nineteen coppers."

Kong Yiji looked up dejectedly and answered, "That ... I'll settle next time. This time I've ready money. The wine must be good."

The tavern keeper, just as usual, said to him with a laugh, "Kong Yiji, you've been stealing again."

But this time he did not protest much, and only said, "Don't make fun of me."

"Make fun of you? If you hadn't been stealing, how would your legs have been broken?"

Kong Yiji said in a low voice, "Fell and broke them, fell, fell ..." His eyes seemed to beg the tavern keeper not to bring it up again. By now several people had gathered, and they all laughed with the tavern keeper. I warmed the wine, carried it out, and set it on the threshold. He took four coppers from his ragged pocket and put them in my hand. His hands were covered with mud; he had come here on those hands. Before long he had finished the wine, and amid the talk and laughter of the others he slowly went away, sitting, using those hands to move.

After that I did not see Kong Yiji for a long time. At the end of the year the tavern keeper took down the board and said, "Kong Yiji still owes nineteen coppers." At the Dragon Boat Festival the next year he said again, "Kong Yiji still owes nineteen coppers." But at the Mid-Autumn Festival he did not mention it, and when the end of the year came round again we still did not see him.

I have never seen him since. Very likely Kong Yiji is really dead.

(March 1919)
