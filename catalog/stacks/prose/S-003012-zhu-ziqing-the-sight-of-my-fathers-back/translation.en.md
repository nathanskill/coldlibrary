It is more than two years since I last saw my father, and what I can least forget is the sight of his back. That winter my grandmother died, and my father lost his post; it was a time when troubles never came singly. I went from Beijing to Xuzhou, intending to go home with my father for the funeral. When I met him in Xuzhou and saw the courtyard strewn with things in disorder, and thought of my grandmother, I could not keep the tears from streaming down my face. My father said, "What's done is done. There's no use grieving. Heaven always leaves us some way out."

At home he sold or pawned things to pay off his debts, and borrowed money for the funeral. In those days the household was in a sorry state, partly because of the funeral and partly because my father was out of work. When the funeral was over, he was to go to Nanjing to look for work, and I was to go back to Beijing to study, so we travelled together.

When we reached Nanjing, some friends invited me out and I stayed on for a day. The next morning I was to cross the river to Pukou and take the train north in the afternoon. My father, being busy, had already decided not to see me off, and had asked a waiter he knew at the hotel to go with me. He gave the waiter instructions over and over again, very carefully. But in the end he did not feel easy, fearing the man would not be reliable, and hesitated for a while. In fact I was twenty that year and had already travelled to and from Beijing two or three times, so there was nothing much to worry about. He hesitated for a while, and in the end decided to take me himself. I urged him two or three times not to go, but he only said, "It doesn't matter. It won't do to send them."

We crossed the river and went into the station. I bought my ticket while he looked after the luggage. There was so much luggage that we had to tip the porters before we could get through, and he busied himself bargaining with them. I was too clever by half in those days: I always felt his way of talking was not quite smart enough, and I had to put in a word. But at last he settled the price and saw me onto the train. He chose a seat for me near the door of the carriage, and I spread on it the purple fur-lined coat he had had made for me. He told me to be careful on the way, to keep awake at night and not to catch cold. He also asked the attendant to look after me well. I laughed to myself at his old-fashioned ways: those people care only for money, and asking them was a waste of breath. Besides, could a grown man like me not look after himself? Ah, when I think of it now, I really was too clever then.

I said, "Father, you can go now." He looked out of the window and said, "I'll go and buy you some tangerines. You stay here. Don't move." I saw several vendors waiting for customers beyond the railings of the platform opposite. To get to that platform he would have to cross the railway line, jumping down onto the tracks and climbing up again. My father was a stout man, so naturally it would be hard for him. I wanted to go myself, but he would not let me, so I had to let him go. I watched him in his little black cloth cap, his black cloth mandarin jacket and his dark blue cotton-padded gown, as he waddled to the edge of the tracks and slowly leaned over and let himself down. That was not too difficult. But when he had crossed the tracks and had to climb up onto the other platform, it was not easy at all. He held on to the top with both hands and drew up his legs, and his stout body leaned slightly to the left, showing what an effort it was. As I watched his back, my tears began to flow at once. I quickly wiped them away, afraid that he or others might see them. When I looked out again, he was already coming back, his arms full of bright red tangerines. When he reached the tracks, he first put the tangerines down on the ground, climbed slowly down himself, then picked them up again and walked on. When he got to this side, I hurried out to help him. He came with me to the train and dumped all the tangerines on my fur coat. Then he brushed the dirt off his clothes, and seemed very relieved. After a while he said, "I'm going now. Write to me when you get there." I watched him walk away. After a few steps he turned his head, saw me, and said, "Go back inside. There's no one there with your things." When his back had mingled with the people coming and going and I could no longer find it, I went back in and sat down, and my tears came again.

In recent years both my father and I have been rushing about from place to place, and things at home have been going from bad to worse. He left home to make his living when he was young, supported himself, and did many big things. Who would have thought his old age would be so dispirited. Everything he saw touched his feelings and saddened him, and naturally he could not control himself. When feelings are pent up inside, they must find a way out; and so trifling household matters often made him angry. He gradually treated me differently from the old days. But in these last two years of not seeing each other, he has at last forgotten my faults, and only thinks of me, and of my son. After I came north, he wrote me a letter, which said: "I am in good health, except that my arm aches badly; it is hard to hold chopsticks or a pen. Probably the time of my great departure is not far off." When I read this, through glistening tears I saw again that stout back in the dark blue padded gown and the black mandarin jacket. Ah, I do not know when I shall see him again.

October, in Beijing.
