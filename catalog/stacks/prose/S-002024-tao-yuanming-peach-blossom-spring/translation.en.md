In the Taiyuan years of the Jin dynasty, there was a man of Wuling who made his living by fishing. He followed a stream and lost track of how far he had gone. Suddenly he came upon a grove of peach trees in blossom, lining both banks for several hundred paces, with no other kind of tree among them. The fragrant grass was fresh and lovely, and fallen petals lay thick and bright. The fisherman was amazed, and went on further, wanting to see where the grove ended.

Where the grove ended, at the source of the stream, he found a hill, and in the hill a small opening from which there seemed to come a glimmer of light. He left his boat and went in through the opening. At first it was very narrow, barely wide enough for a person. After a few dozen more paces it suddenly opened out into a broad, bright space. The land was flat and wide, and the houses stood in neat rows. There were good fields, fine ponds, mulberry trees and bamboo. Paths ran between the fields in every direction, and the sound of roosters and dogs carried from one to the next. The men and women coming and going and working in the fields were dressed just like people outside. The old with their yellowed hair and the children with their tufts were all content and happy.

When they saw the fisherman they were greatly surprised, and asked where he had come from. He told them everything. They invited him to their homes, set out wine, killed a chicken and made a meal for him. When the people of the village heard about him, they all came to ask him questions. They said that their ancestors had fled the troubles of the Qin dynasty and had brought their wives, children and neighbours to this remote place, and had never gone out again, and so had been cut off from the people outside. They asked what age it was now. They did not even know there had been a Han dynasty, let alone the Wei and the Jin. The fisherman told them, one by one, everything he had heard, and they all sighed. The others each invited him to their homes in turn and brought out wine and food. After staying several days he took his leave. The people there told him: "It is not worth speaking of this to the people outside."

When he came out he found his boat and went back along the way he had come, marking the places as he went. When he reached the prefectural town he went to see the governor and told him all this. The governor at once sent people to go back with him and look for the marks he had made, but they lost their way and could not find the path again.

Liu Ziji of Nanyang was a scholar of lofty character. When he heard of this he gladly made plans to go there. Before he could do so he fell ill and passed away. After that no one asked the way to the ford.

## The Poem of Peach Blossom Spring

When the Ying clan threw heaven's order into chaos, worthy men withdrew from that age. Huang and Qi went to Mount Shang, and these people too went away.

The traces of their going sank slowly out of sight, the path they took grew wild and was lost. They called on one another to work the fields, and at sunset each went home to rest.

Mulberry and bamboo cast their ample shade, beans and millet were sown in their season. In spring the silkworms gave long threads; at the autumn harvest there was no king's tax.

Overgrown paths dimly crossed and met, roosters and dogs called back and forth. Their sacrificial vessels kept the ancient forms, their clothes were made in no new style.

Children wandered freely, singing; the white-haired went visiting, glad. When grass grew lush they knew the season was mild; when trees grew bare they knew the wind was sharp.

Though they had no calendar or records, the four seasons made a year of themselves. Content, with joy to spare, what need had they to labour with cleverness?

These rare traces stayed hidden five hundred years, then one morning that hidden world lay open. But the pure and the shallow come from different springs, and soon it was closed and hidden once more.

May I ask you, who roam within the world: how can you fathom what lies beyond its dust and noise? I would like to tread the light wind and rise high to find those of my own kind.
