In the twelfth month of the fifth year of Chongzhen, I was living by the West Lake. It had snowed heavily for three days, and on the lake every sound of people and birds had vanished.

That day, after the first watch of the night had been struck, I took a small boat and, wrapped in a fur coat with a little stove of coals, went alone to the pavilion in the middle of the lake to look at the snow. The frozen mist hung everywhere, and sky and cloud and hills and water were one whiteness above and below. The only shadows on the lake were a single stroke, the long causeway; a single dot, the mid-lake pavilion; my boat, a mustard seed; and the people in it, two or three grains.

When I reached the pavilion, there were two people sitting on a spread felt rug, facing each other, while a boy warmed wine and the stove was just coming to the boil. When they saw me they were overjoyed and said: "How can there be another person like this on the lake?" They pulled me in to drink with them. I made myself drink three large cups, and then took my leave. I asked their names: they were from Jinling, staying here as guests.

When I got back down into the boat, the boatman muttered: "Don't call the master mad. There are others as mad as the master."
