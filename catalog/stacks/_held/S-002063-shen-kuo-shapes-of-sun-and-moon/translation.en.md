When I was editing texts in the Zhaowen Library, I took part in the review of the armillary sphere. The official in charge asked me: "Of the twenty-eight lunar lodges, the largest spans thirty-three degrees and the smallest only one. Why are they so unequal?" I replied: "In the heavens themselves there are no degrees. Those who calculate the calendar had nowhere to lodge their numbers, so they took the sun's path to divide the sky into three hundred and sixty-five degrees and a fraction. The sun moves evenly and completes its circuit of the sky in a little more than three hundred and sixty-five days, so one day was taken as one degree. Once the sky had been divided, there had to be something to mark the divisions, so that they could be observed and counted. So the stars that fall exactly on the degree lines were used as markers. Along the ecliptic, in the course of the sun's yearly journey, the stars that fall on these lines are only the stars of the twenty-eight lodges. The degrees are like the ribs of an umbrella, and the stars on the degree lines are those that sit exactly on the ribs. That is why a carriage canopy has twenty-eight ribs, to represent the twenty-eight lodges. As I wrote in my memorial on the armillary sphere: 'The degrees cannot be seen; what can be seen are the stars. Along the paths of the sun, the moon and the five planets there are stars. Those that fall on the marked lines number twenty-eight, and are called lodges. The lodges hold up the degrees, and the degrees give rise to numbers.' These are what are now called the measuring stars. It is not that no one wanted them to be equal. Along the ecliptic, these are the only stars that fall on the lines."

He also asked me: "Are the sun and moon shaped like balls, or like fans? If they are like balls, would they not get in each other's way when they meet?" I replied: "The sun and moon are shaped like balls. How do we know? We can prove it from the waxing and waning of the moon. The moon has no light of its own; it is like a silver ball, and shines only when the sun lights it. When the light first appears, the sun is beside it, so the light falls on its side and what we see is only like a hook. As the sun moves further away, it shines on the moon at a slant, and the light gradually fills out. Take a ball and coat half of it with white powder. Look at it from the side, and the powdered part is like a hook; look at it head on, and it is perfectly round. By this we know it is like a ball. The sun and moon are made of breath, of qi: they have form but no substance, so when they meet they do not get in each other's way."

He also asked: "The sun and moon come together once and stand opposite once every month. Why is there sometimes an eclipse and sometimes not?" I replied: "The ecliptic and the moon's path are like two rings laid one on top of the other, slightly askew. Whenever the sun and moon meet in the same degree, the sun is eclipsed; whenever they stand exactly opposite in the same degree, the moon is somewhat darkened. But even when they are in the same degree, if the moon's path is not close to the ecliptic, they naturally do not interfere with each other. Only when they are in the same degree and also near a crossing point of the ecliptic and the moon's path do the sun and moon meet and cover one another. If it is exactly at the crossing, the eclipse is total; if not exactly at the crossing, the eclipse is deep or shallow according to how far they overlap. In a solar eclipse, when the moon's path is crossing from outside to inside, the eclipse begins in the southwest and ends in the northeast; when it is crossing from inside to outside, it begins in the northwest and ends in the southeast. When the sun is east of the crossing, the inner side is eclipsed; when the sun is west of the crossing, the outer side is eclipsed. A total eclipse begins due west and ends due east. In a lunar eclipse, when the moon's path is crossing from outside to inside, it begins in the southeast and ends in the northwest; when crossing from inside to outside, it begins in the northeast and ends in the southwest. When the moon is east of the crossing, its outer side is eclipsed; when west of the crossing, its inner side is eclipsed. A total eclipse begins due east and ends in the west. The crossing points move back a little more than a degree each month, and after two hundred and forty-nine crossings they complete a full circuit. That is why the Indian methods count Rahu and Ketu backwards: they are what we now call the crossing points. The first crossing is called Rahu, and the middle crossing is called Ketu."
