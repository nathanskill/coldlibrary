Heaven covers and earth bears up, and things are counted in their ten thousands; and the work of making them is accomplished in every detail, with nothing left out. Can this be the strength of people alone?

Since things and works number in the ten thousands, if we had to wait to be told by word of mouth and shown with our own eyes before we understood them, how many could we ever know? Of all these ten thousand works and things, those that are of no benefit to human life and those that are of benefit are about half and half.

There are clever and widely read people in the world whom the crowd looks up to. Yet they have never looked at the blossom of the jujube or the pear, while they speculate about the "fruit of the Chu river weed"; they have hardly ever seen the mould for an iron cauldron, while they hold forth about the "tripod of Ju". Painters love to paint ghosts and demons and dislike painting dogs and horses. Even a Zichan of Zheng or a Zhang Hua of Jin, what credit would that bring them?

I am fortunate to live in an enlightened and flourishing age, when carts and horses from southern Yunnan travel all the way to Liaoyang, and officials and merchants from the passes of the far south roam freely in the north. Within ten thousand miles, what work or thing cannot be seen and heard? If a scholar had been born at the beginning of the Eastern Jin or at the end of the Southern Song, the products of Yan, Qin, Jin and Yu would all have become foreign goods to him, and furs and caps would have been obtained only through trade at the frontier. How would they have differed from the arrows of the Sushen? Besides, princes and royal children born and raised in the depths of the palace, while the rice in the imperial kitchen is fragrant, may wish to see a plough; while the palace silks are freshly cut, they may try to imagine a loom. At such a time, to open a book of pictures and look would be like finding a great treasure.

In recent years I have written a book called The Works of Heaven and the Inception of Things. Alas for poverty. I wanted to buy rare things to test and verify, but lacked the means of a Luoyang household; I wanted to invite like-minded friends to discuss what was genuine and what was false, but lacked a hall like that of Cao Zhi, Prince of Chen. I could only write down what my own narrow experience had seen and heard and kept in my heart. How could it be adequate? My friend Mr Tu Boju is sincere enough to move heaven, and his mind reaches into the nature of things. Wherever there is a good word, old or new, or the smallest merit worth taking, he is sure to take it up warmly and diligently. Last year it was through him that my Huayin guizheng, a book on the sounds of characters, was sent to the printer, and now, at his further bidding, he has taken up this book and is having it printed too. Is this not something brought about by an old bond? The book is divided into a first and a last part, in the spirit of "honour the five grains and think little of gold and jade". Two chapters, on observing the heavens and on music and pitch, dealt with matters too subtle for me; judging that they were not my business, I removed them just before printing.

Let the ambitious men of letters toss it aside on their desks: this book has nothing whatever to do with advancement in examinations and office.

Written by Song Yingxing of Fengxin in the first month of summer of the year dingchou of Chongzhen, in the Hall of the Question of Eating at Home.
