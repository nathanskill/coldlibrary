That year the Son of Heaven first performed the Feng sacrifice for the house of Han; but the Grand Historian was held back at Zhounan and could not take part, and in his frustration he fell ill and was near his end. His son Qian, just then returning from a mission, found his father between the Yellow River and the Luo. The Grand Historian took Qian's hand and wept, and said: "Our forebears were grand historians to the house of Zhou. From the highest antiquity they were renowned under Yu and Xia, and had charge of the affairs of heaven. In later ages the line declined; is it to end with me? If you in your turn become grand historian, you will carry on our ancestors. Now the Son of Heaven, heir to a line of a thousand years, performs the Feng sacrifice on Mount Tai, and I cannot go with him. This is fate; this is fate! When I am gone you must become grand historian, and when you are grand historian, do not forget what I wished to set out and write. Filial piety begins in serving one's parents, goes on in serving one's lord, and ends in establishing oneself; to make one's name known to later ages and so bring honour to one's father and mother is the greatest filial piety. The whole world praises the Duke of Zhou because he could set forth in song the virtue of Kings Wen and Wu, spread the ways of Zhou and Shao, convey the thoughts of the Great King and Wang Ji, and reach back to Gong Liu, to honour Hou Ji. After Kings You and Li the kingly way fell short and rites and music decayed; Confucius restored the old and revived what had lapsed, discussed the Songs and the Documents and made the Spring and Autumn Annals, and scholars have taken them as their rule to this day. Since the capture of the unicorn more than four hundred years have passed; the lords swallowed one another, and the records of the historians were scattered and cut off. Now the Han has risen and all within the seas is one. Enlightened rulers and worthy sovereigns, loyal ministers and men who died for what was right: if I, as grand historian, fail to discuss and record them, and let the historical writing of the world lapse, I shall be greatly afraid. Think of it!" Qian bowed his head and wept, and said: "Your son is not clever, but I ask to set out in full the old accounts my father arranged, and I will not dare to leave anything out."

Three years after his father's death Qian became Grand Historian, and drew out and arranged the records and writings kept in the stone chambers and metal caskets. Five years later came the first year of Taichu: at dawn on the jiazi day that began the eleventh month, the day of the winter solstice, the calendar of heaven was first reformed; it was proclaimed in the Hall of Light, and all the spirits received the new reckoning.

The Grand Historian says: "My father used to say: 'Five hundred years after the Duke of Zhou died there was Confucius. From the death of Confucius to today it is five hundred years. Is there one who can carry on the brightness of that age, set right the commentaries on the Changes, continue the Spring and Autumn, and ground himself in the Songs, the Documents, the Rites and Music?' Was this what he meant? Was this what he meant? How dare I, his son, decline?"

* * *

Our Han carries on from the last currents of the Five Emperors and takes up the broken work of the Three Dynasties. When the way of Zhou declined, Qin threw out the ancient script and burned the Songs and the Documents, and so the charts and records of the Hall of Light, the stone chambers, the metal caskets and the jade tablets were scattered and thrown into disorder. Then the Han arose. Xiao He arranged the statutes and ordinances, Han Xin set out the laws of war, Zhang Cang drew up the regulations and standards, and Shusun Tong fixed the rites and ceremonies; learning and letters little by little flourished again, and the Songs and the Documents came to light here and there. After Cao Can recommended Master Gai, who spoke for the Yellow Emperor and Laozi, and Jia Yi and Chao Cuo expounded Shen Buhai and Lord Shang, and Gongsun Hong rose to eminence as a Confucian, within a hundred years the surviving writings and old affairs of the world had all been gathered to the Grand Historian. Father and son held the office of Grand Historian in turn and carried on its work. He said: "Ah! My forebears once had charge of this work and were eminent under Tang and Yu; under Zhou they held it again, so that the Sima family has had charge of the affairs of heaven for generations. Now it has come to me. Let me keep it in mind! Let me keep it in mind!" I gathered up the old accounts scattered and lost throughout the world. Where the deeds of kings arose, I traced them from their beginnings to their ends, saw them flourishing and watched them decline, and discussed and examined them against what was done. I gave a brief account of the Three Dynasties and a record of Qin and Han, from Xuanyuan at the top down to the present, and wrote twelve Basic Annals, set out under their headings. Where events were of one time but in different states, and the years did not agree, I made ten Tables. For the gains and losses of rites and music, the changes of pitch-pipes and calendar, military power, mountains and rivers, spirits and gods, the boundary of heaven and man, and how each age, inheriting the faults of the last, found its way through change, I made eight Treatises. As the twenty-eight lodges of the moon circle the Northern Pole, and thirty spokes share one hub, turning without end, so the ministers who are the arms and legs of the throne are matched to it, loyal and trustworthy in following the way to serve their ruler: I made thirty Hereditary Houses. For those who upheld what was right, free and extraordinary, who did not let their moment pass and won achievement and fame in the world, I made seventy Biographies. In all a hundred and thirty chapters, five hundred and twenty-six thousand five hundred characters: the Book of the Grand Historian. I have set it out in brief, to gather what was missed and fill what was lacking, to found a school of my own, to bring into accord the differing commentaries on the Six Classics and put in order the miscellaneous sayings of the Hundred Schools. It is to be stored in a famous mountain, with a copy in the capital, to await the sages and gentlemen of later ages. The seventieth chapter.

The Grand Historian says: I have recounted from the Yellow Emperor down to the Taichu era, and there I stop: a hundred and thirty chapters.
