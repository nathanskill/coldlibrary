Mount Xian stands above the Han river. Seen from a distance it is faint and low, the least of the hills, and yet its name is especially famous in Jingzhou: surely because of its people! And who are they? Yang Hu, styled Shuzi, and Du Yu, styled Yuankai. When Jin and Wu were contending in arms, both relied on Jingzhou as their mainstay, and these two held it one after the other, until Wu was conquered and the work of Jin was complete. Their achievements already overshadowed their own age; and the grace and lingering charm of their manner, which spread so gently over the lands of the Yangzi and the Han, people still think of today, and think of Shuzi most deeply of all. For Yuankai is remembered for his achievements and Shuzi for his kindness; what the two did was not the same, yet both are enough to be handed down as things that do not decay.

I rather wonder, then, why they were themselves so anxious about their names in later ages. It is said that Shuzi once climbed this mountain and said to his staff, with a sigh, that the mountain was always here, while the men of earlier ages had all vanished without a name; and he looked at himself and grieved. He did not see that the mountain would wait for him to make it famous. Yuankai had his achievements inscribed on two stones: one he set on top of this mountain, and one he threw into the depths of the Han. So he knew that hills and valleys change places, but not that stone, too, wears away in its time. Were they both so pleased with their names that they took too much thought for an endless future? Or did they ask much of themselves, and think a long way ahead?

There was once a pavilion on the mountain, said to be where Shuzi used to stop on his walks. That it has so often fallen and been rebuilt is because so many in later ages have admired his name and thought of the man. In the first year of Xining my friend Shi Zhonghui came to govern Xiangyang with the rank of Minister of the Imperial Household, and the next year he enlarged and renewed the old pavilion, surrounding it with a fine covered walk and enlarging the hall behind it to match. He is well known in our time and has a good name wherever he goes; the people of Xiang are content with his government and glad to go walking with him. So they named the hall after his office, the Hall of the Minister, and wanted to record the matter on a stone, so that it would be handed down with the names of Shuzi and Yuankai for a long time to come; he could not stop them, and they came to ask me for the record. I say that he admires the manner of Shuzi and follows in his footsteps, so what kind of man he is and where his purpose lies can be known; and the people of Xiang love him and are at peace under him, so how he governs Xiang can be known too. This is what the people of Xiang wish to have written.

As for the splendid lie of the hills and rivers around it, and the haze of grass, trees, cloud and smoke appearing and vanishing in the empty distance, which could serve a poet climbing high or express the far gaze of the poet of the Sao, those who look will find that for themselves. And as for how often the pavilion has fallen and risen, whether it has its own records or need not be traced in detail, I shall say no more.

Written by the Retired Scholar of the Six Ones, Ouyang Xiu, on the twenty-second day of the tenth month of the third year of Xining.
