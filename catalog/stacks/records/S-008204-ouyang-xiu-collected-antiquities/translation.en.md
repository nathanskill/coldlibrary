Things gather around those who love them, and are obtained by those who have the strength. With strength but no love, or love but no strength, a thing may be near and easy and still not be obtained. Elephants and rhinoceros, tigers and leopards are man-killing beasts of the wild lands by the mountains and the seas, yet their tusks, horns, hides and skins can be gathered and owned. Jade comes from beyond the Kunlun mountains and the Shifting Sands, ten thousand li away, and passes through more than ten interpreters before it reaches the Middle Kingdom. Pearls come from the southern sea and grow in deep abysses; the gatherers go into the water on ropes tied round their waists and come up hardly looking human, and often they do not come up at all but feed the dragons and fish below. Gold is mined in the mountains: men dig deep and tunnel far and go in with caged fires and dried rations, and when a cliff gives way or a shaft is blocked, they are buried inside it, often by the dozen or the hundred. Such is the distance and the hardship, and the many deaths; yet the world commonly gathers and owns gold and jade, pearls and gems together. Whatever is loved by those who have the strength will always arrive.

The basin of King Tang and the tripod of the Kong family, the stone drums of Qiyang, the carved stones of Mount Tai, Mount Yi and Kuaiji, together with the stelae and ritual bronzes, inscriptions, poems, prefaces and records of sage rulers and worthy men since Han and Wei, and down to the calligraphy of the masters of the ancient script, the large and small seal, the bafen and the clerical script: these are the most precious, strange, splendid, fine and delightful things left from the Three Dynasties onward. They are not far from us, and taking them brings no danger; yet wind and frost, war and fire have sunk them and worn them away and scattered them among mountain cliffs and overgrown ruins, and no one has gathered them up, because few in the world love them. And if by good fortune someone does love them, his strength may not be enough, so that he gets only one or two of them and cannot bring them together.

Strength is not as good as love, and love is not as good as single-mindedness. I am stubborn by nature and fond of antiquity, and I have no desire for anything the world covets, so I could give all my love to this one thing. When love is deep enough, even if strength falls short one can still obtain things. So from King Mu of Zhou onward, down through Qin, Han, Sui, Tang and the Five Dynasties, and outward to the four seas and the nine provinces, the famous mountains and great marshes, the remotest cliffs and the deepest valleys, wild forests and broken tombs, gods and immortals, ghosts and spirits, and whatever strange tales have been passed down, there is nothing that is not here; and of it I made the Record of Collected Antiquities. Believing that copies made by hand lose the truth, I kept the rubbings taken from the stones themselves, mounted them on rollers and stored them. They are in order of volume and case, not in order of their age, because I kept acquiring more and so recorded them as they came. Believing also that what is gathered in quantity must in the end be scattered, I picked out their main points and made a separate catalogue, and set down in it whatever can correct the gaps and errors of the histories, to hand on to later scholars, in the hope that it will add to what they know.

Someone mocked me: "When things are many they are hard to keep together, and what has long been gathered is always scattered. Why take such trouble over this?" I answered: "It is enough that I satisfy what I love and grow old enjoying it. Can a hoard of ivory, rhinoceros horn, gold and jade really escape being scattered? I am not yet able to exchange this for that."
