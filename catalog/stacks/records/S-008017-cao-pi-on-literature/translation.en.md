Men of letters look down on one another; it has been so since antiquity. Between Fu Yi and Ban Gu there was as little to choose as between an elder and a younger brother, yet Ban Gu belittled him. He wrote to his brother Chao: "Wuzhong became an archivist at the Orchid Terrace because he could compose; but once he picks up his brush he cannot stop." People are good at seeing their own strengths, but writing has more than one form, and few are good at all of them. So each, from what he does well, scorns what another does poorly. The village saying goes: "A worn-out broom at home is valued at a thousand in gold." That is the trouble with not seeing oneself. Among the writers of our day there are Kong Rong, styled Wenju, of Lu; Chen Lin, styled Kongzhang, of Guangling; Wang Can, styled Zhongxuan, of Shanyang; Xu Gan, styled Weichang, of Beihai; Ruan Yu, styled Yuanyu, of Chenliu; Ying Yang, styled Delian, of Runan; and Liu Zhen, styled Gonggan, of Dongping. These seven have left nothing out of their learning and borrowed nothing in their language. Each believes he is galloping a thoroughbred over a thousand li, head high, keeping pace and racing abreast with the rest. For such men to defer to one another is hard indeed. A gentleman examines himself before he measures others, and so escapes this burden; and so I write this essay on literature.

Wang Can excels at rhapsodies. Xu Gan at times has the slow manner of Qi, but he is Wang Can's match. Wang Can's "First Campaign," "Climbing the Tower," "The Locust Tree" and "Thoughts on Campaign," and Xu Gan's "Black Gibbon," "Leaking Goblet," "Round Fan" and "The Orange" are not surpassed even by Zhang Heng and Cai Yong. In other forms, though, they do not reach that height. The memorials, petitions, letters and records of Chen Lin and Ruan Yu are the finest of our time. Ying Yang is harmonious but lacks strength; Liu Zhen is strong but lacks fineness. Kong Rong's style and spirit are lofty and subtle, with something no one else has; but he cannot sustain an argument, his reasoning falls short of his phrasing, and he goes so far as to mix in mockery and jest. At his best he is the peer of Yang Xiong and Ban Gu.

Ordinary people prize what is far away and despise what is near; they turn toward reputation and away from substance. They also suffer from being blind to themselves, and think themselves worthy. Writing is one at the root and different in its branches. Memorials and deliberations should be dignified; letters and treatises should be reasoned; inscriptions and eulogies value truth; poems and rhapsodies aim at beauty. These four kinds differ, and so those who are able in them are partial. Only a talent that reaches through all of them can master every form.

In writing, the breath of the writer is the master. That breath is clear or turbid by its own nature, and cannot be had by forcing. Compare it to music: the tune may be the same and the rhythm kept to the same measure, but in the drawing of breath no two players are alike, and skill and clumsiness are there from the start. Even a father or an elder brother cannot hand them on to a son or a younger brother.

For writing is the great work of ordering a state, a splendid undertaking that does not decay. A life ends when its years are spent; honour and pleasure stop with the person. Both come to their appointed end, and neither is like writing, which has none. So the writers of old lodged themselves in ink and brush and showed their minds in chapters and books. They borrowed no fine historian's words and leaned on no swift and powerful patron, and still their names passed on to later ages of their own accord. So the Lord of the West, held captive, worked out the Changes, and the Duke of Zhou, in his eminence, set down the Rites: the one did not give up the work because he was in hardship, and the other did not let ease draw his mind away. That being so, the ancients scorned a jade disc a foot across and prized an inch of shadow on the sundial, afraid that time would pass them by. Yet most people do not exert themselves. Poor and humble, they are cowed by hunger and cold; rich and noble, they drift into idle pleasure. So they busy themselves with what is in front of them and leave undone the work of a thousand years. Sun and moon go by above, body and face wear away below, and suddenly one is changed along with all the ten thousand things. This is the great sorrow of a man with a purpose. Kong Rong and the others are gone. Only Xu Gan wrote a treatise and made a school of his own.
