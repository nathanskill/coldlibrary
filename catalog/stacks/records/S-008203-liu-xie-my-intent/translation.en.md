"The literary mind" means the care that goes into writing. In former times there were Juanzi's Mind of the Zither and Wangsun's Mind of Skill. The mind is a beautiful thing, and so I have used the word. Writing has always taken its form through carving and rich ornament; I do not take "dragon-carving" merely from what the crowd once said of Zou Shi.

The universe stretches on without end, and the many and the worthy are mixed together; what raises one above the rest is intelligence and skill, nothing more. The years and months fly by, and the spirit does not stay; what sends a name aloft and makes its substance fly is making things, nothing more. A human being has a form like heaven and earth and a nature drawn from the five elements, eyes and ears modelled on the sun and moon, voice and breath like the wind and thunder; in rising above the ten thousand things he is spiritual indeed. His body is as frail as grass and trees, but a name can outlast the hardness of metal and stone. So the gentleman, in his time in the world, establishes virtue and establishes words. Is it that he loves argument? He has no choice.

When I was seven I dreamed of clouds like coloured brocade, and climbed up to gather them. Past thirty, I dreamed one night that I was carrying the red-lacquered vessels of the rites and following Confucius south. When I woke at dawn I was glad. How great he is, the sage so hard to see, and yet he came down into the dream of a young man like me! Since humankind was born there has been no one like the Master.

To set forth and praise the sage's meaning, nothing is better than commenting on the classics; but Ma Rong, Zheng Xuan and the other scholars have expounded them thoroughly, and even if I reached some deep understanding it would not be enough to found a school. Yet the uses of writing are truly branches of the classics. The five kinds of rites depend on it to be complete, and the six departments of government on it to work; by it ruler and minister shine, and by it the affairs of army and state are made clear. Trace them to their source and all come from the classics. But we are far from the sage now. The forms of writing have come apart; writers love the strange and prize the floating and fantastic, decorating feathers and painting them, embroidering girdles and kerchiefs. They stray further and further from the root and will soon fall into error and excess. The Book of Zhou, speaking of language, values the essential; Confucius in his teaching disliked heresies. Language and teaching differ, but both should keep to what is essential. So I took up the brush and mixed the ink, and began to write about literature.

When I look closely at those in recent times who have written about literature, they are many: Emperor Wen of Wei's Discourse, the Prince of Chen's Letter, Ying Yang's On Literature, Lu Ji's Rhapsody on Literature, Zhongqia's Divisions, Hongfan's Forest of Brushes. Each lights up a corner or a crack, and few see the open highway. Some judge the talents of their own time, some weigh the writing of earlier masters, some range broadly over the refined and the vulgar, some sum up the ideas of particular works. The Discourse of Wei is close-knit but not complete; the Letter of Chen is eloquent but off the mark; Ying's essay is splendid but sketchy; Lu's rhapsody is skilful but fragmented; the Divisions is precise but achieves little; the Forest of Brushes is shallow and short of essentials. Then there are men like Junshan and Gonggan, Jifu and Shilong, who discuss the meaning of writing in general terms, and their remarks turn up from time to time; but none of them could shake the leaves to find the root, or watch the waves to seek the source. If one does not pass on the teachings of the wise who went before, one is no help to the thinking of those born after.

The Literary Mind is rooted in the Way, takes the sage as teacher and the classics as its body, draws on the apocrypha and transforms the Sao: the pivot of writing is here, as far as it can reach. In discussing rhymed and unrhymed writing, each kind has its own field and division: I trace origins to show their later course, explain names to make their meaning plain, choose works to fix the canon, and set out principles to bring them together. In the first part the main lines are clear. As for dissecting feeling and analysing colour, enclosing and connecting the threads, I describe spirit and nature, picture wind and force, take in continuity and change, and examine sound and word; I trace rise and fall in "The Times", praise and blame in "Talents", lament in "The One Who Knows the Music", stand upright in "The Vessel", and in "My Intent" I gather my long thoughts to rule all the chapters. In the second part the fine details are plain. In arranging the principles and fixing the names I follow the number of the Great Changes: the chapters put to use on writing are only forty-nine.

To arrange and order one piece of writing is easy; to embrace the whole range of what has been said is hard. Though I have picked up the lightest hairs and probed to the marrow, there are crooked meanings and hidden springs that seem near but are far, and what my words do not record is past counting. Where my judgements of the finished works agree with old opinions, it is not that I echo them: the case simply cannot be otherwise. Where they differ from earlier views, it is not that I differ for the sake of it: the reasoning simply cannot be the same. Agreeing or differing, I pay no heed to old or new; I cut along the grain of the flesh and seek only the balanced mean. Holding the reins in the field of refined letters, circling the storehouse of ornament, I have come close to covering it all. But words cannot fully say what is meant, a thing even the sage found hard; and my knowledge is that of a jar or a pipe, how could it be a carpenter's rule and square? The ages past, vast and dim, have already steeped my hearing; the generations to come, far and faint, perhaps this will lie like dust before their eyes.

The eulogy says: Life has its limit; knowledge alone has none. To chase after things is truly hard; to follow one's nature is easy. Proud among the springs and rocks, I chew on the meaning of writing. If writing truly carries the mind, my mind has a place to lodge.
