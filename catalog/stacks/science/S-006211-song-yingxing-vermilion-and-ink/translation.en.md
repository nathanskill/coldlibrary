Master Song says: That letters have not fallen away in a thousand ages is owed to setting down black on white; what work can be compared with it? Out of the red fire of Li the deepest black is conceived; out of white mercury the purest red appears by transformation. In the furnace and hammer of creation, what room is there for our reasoning? When the five-coloured decrees come down from on high, vermilion beside the black makes the great titles shine; when ten thousand scrolls lie open, the black, set off by vermilion, makes the writing of heaven glow. Among the rare treasures of the scholar’s desk, what need is there of pearls and jade? And when painters portray all things, taking now the natural colours and now mixtures of them, every colour is there. These too depend on water and cling to fire, together showing the changing forms of the Five Phases. Who but the most divine could have a hand in this?

## Vermilion

Cinnabar, mercury and silver vermilion are at bottom one substance; they have different names according to how fine or coarse, how mature or young they are. The best cinnabar, from Chenjin (now called Mayang) and from western Sichuan, holds mercury within it, but it is not sublimed. For the bright, the arrowhead and the mirror-face kinds of cinnabar are worth three times their weight in mercury, so they are picked out and sold as cinnabar; subliming them into mercury would lower their value. Only coarse and inferior cinnabar is sublimed into mercury, and the mercury is then sublimed again into silver vermilion.

The finest cinnabar is reached by digging ten zhang and more into the ground. Its first sign is a heap of white rock, which is called the cinnabar bed. The cinnabar near the bed may be as big as a hen’s egg. The next grade of cinnabar is not used in medicine; it is only ground for painters or sublimed into mercury. Its sign need not be white rock, and it can be found a few zhang down. Around the bed there may be green or yellow stone, or sand and earth in between; when the earth is full of it, the sand and stone around it often crack of themselves. Cinnabar of this kind is most plentiful in Sizhou, Yinjiang and Tongren in Guizhou, and it also comes in quantity from Shangzhou and Qinzhou. When the second-grade cinnabar is brought out, if the whole pit is whitish and young in colour, it is not ground for vermilion but all sublimed into mercury. If the cinnabar is young but one wants to refine it to red, then as soon as it is brought out it goes into a great iron trough-mill and is crushed as fine as dust, then put into a vat and soaked and settled in clear water. After three days and nights, what floats on top is skimmed off and poured into another vat, and this is called second vermilion. What sinks and settles at the bottom is dried in the sun and called first vermilion.

To sublime mercury, one uses either young, white second-grade cinnabar or the second vermilion skimmed from the vat. It is mixed with water and rolled into large coiled strips, and thirty catties go into a cauldron to sublime the mercury, with thirty catties of charcoal beneath. For the sublimation another cauldron is set on top, with a small hole left in its centre, and the edges of the cauldrons are sealed tight with salt-mud. On the upper cauldron an iron tube bent like a bow is fitted; the tube is wound along its whole length with hemp cord and coated over with salt-mud as well. When the fire is lit, one end of the bent tube goes into the cauldron to carry off the vapour (where it goes in, it is sealed without the slightest gap); for the other end, a medium-sized jar holds two bottles of water and the tail of the tube is put into it, so that the vapour from the cauldron travels as far as the water in the jar and stops. After firing for five double-hours, all the cinnabar powder inside has turned into mercury, spread over the whole cauldron. It is left to cool for a day, then taken out and swept down. This is the most marvellous mystery: the whole working of heaven is in this change. (The Bencao’s commentary is nonsense: “dig a hole in the ground and set a bowl of water in it.”)

Mercury sublimed again to make vermilion is for that reason called silver vermilion. For the method one uses either a clay jar with a mouth like a chime-stone, or an upper and a lower cauldron. To each catty of mercury two catties of shitingzhi (that is, prepared sulphur) are added; they are ground together until no glints can be seen, and roasted into a black powder, which is packed into the jar. It is covered with an iron dish, and an iron ruler is laid on the dish as a weight. It is bound with iron wire round under the base, the seams of the mouth are sealed with salt-mud, and below, three nails are driven into the ground to hold the jar like the legs of a tripod. The fire is kept up for as long as three sticks of incense take to burn, and the dish is wiped often with an old brush dipped in water; the mercury then turns into powder of itself and sticks to the jar, and what sticks near the mouth is the brightest vermilion of all. When it has cooled the cover is lifted, and the vermilion is scraped and swept off for use. The shitingzhi sinks to the bottom of the jar and can be taken out and used again. Each catty of mercury sublimed gives fourteen ounces of vermilion and three ounces five qian of second vermilion; the extra weight comes from the sulphur. Sublimed vermilion and ground vermilion serve much the same uses. But for the paintings of the imperial and noble houses, vermilion ground from the cinnabar of Chenjin is used, not this kind. Vermilion for the scholar’s desk is made up with glue into sticks and cakes; on a stone inkstone it shows well, but ground on a tin inkstone it turns at once into a black liquid. And when lacquerers colour things brightly, it shows only when mixed with tung oil; put into lacquer it goes dull.

Mercury and vermilion come from nowhere else. The tales of “seas of mercury” and “mercury from plants” are wild nonsense, believed by those who take hearsay for knowledge. Once mercury has been sublimed into vermilion it cannot be turned back into mercury: that is where the skill of creation comes to its end.

## Ink

Ink is made by burning soot and setting it into a solid. Ink from the soot of tung oil, vegetable oil and lard makes up one part in ten; ink from pine soot makes up nine parts in ten. For the making of precious ink our dynasty holds the people of Huizhou in the highest regard; because oil is hard to carry, some of them send men to live in Jing, Xiang, Chen and Yuan, where they burn the cheap tung oil for its soot and bring it home. When such ink is put on paper and the sun strikes across it, if it shows a red gleam, the lamp wicks were soaked in the juice of gromwell before they were lit.

When oil is burned for its soot, each catty of oil gives a little over an ounce of the best soot. A man who is quick with his hands can tend two hundred lamps. If he is slow to scrape, the soot grows old, and both the fire and the material are wasted. For ordinary ink, the pine trees are first drained of their resin and then felled. If the least hair of resin is left in, ink made from the soot will always have the fault of lumps that will not dissolve. To drain the resin from a pine, a small hole is cut at the root and a lamp is set burning there to warm it slowly; then the oil of the whole tree flows out toward the warmth.

To burn pine soot, the pines are felled and cut into lengths. A round chamber is made of bent bamboo strips, shaped like the rain-awning of a boat, and joined end to end for ten zhang and more. Inside and out, and at the joins, it is pasted tight with paper and matting. Every few sections a small hole is left for the smoke, and underneath, earth and bricks are laid beforehand as a passage for the smoke. The wood is burned for several days; when it has cooled, men go inside to sweep and scrape. In burning pine soot, the fire is lit and the smoke passes from the head to the tail. The soot from the one or two sections nearest the tail is clear soot, taken as material for fine ink. That from the middle sections is mixed soot, taken as material for ordinary ink. From the one or two sections near the head it is only scraped off as soot-powder and sold to the printers of books, who still grind it fine before they use it. The rest goes to the lacquerers and plasterers for black coatings.

When ink is made from pine soot, the soot is soaked in water for a long time, and the clear is told from the coarse by what floats and what sinks. After the glue is mixed in, the amount of hammering decides whether it will be brittle or hard. As for adding precious ingredients, washing it with gold or steeping it with musk, and how much pine soot or oil soot to use, that is left to the maker. For the rest, the Classic of Ink and the Ink Register are there for those who want to know more; this is no more than a rough record of the materials and where they come from.
