Shennong made the plough and its share to benefit the world; Yao charged his four officers to give the people the seasons with reverence; Shun commanded Hou Ji, putting food first among the affairs of government; Yu laid out the land and the fields, and the ten thousand states were set in order. The flourishing of Yin and Zhou, as the Songs and Documents describe it, came down to giving the people security, making them prosperous and then teaching them.

Shennong and Cangjie were sages, yet in practical matters there were things beyond them. So Zhao Guo was the first to plough with oxen, a real gain on the hand plough; and Cai Lun conceived the making of paper, which spared the trouble of silk and wooden tablets. Geng Shouchang’s ever-normal granaries and Sang Hongyang’s system of equal transport profited the state and the people: these are arts that do not decay. The proverb says: “Wise as Yu and Tang, but not as good as having tried it.” That is why, when Fan Chi asked to learn farming, Confucius answered: “I am not as good as an old farmer.” If even the wisdom of sages and worthies falls short somewhere, how much more that of ordinary people?

Yidun, a poor scholar of Lu, heard that Lord Taozhu was rich and asked him how. He was told: “If you want to get rich quickly, keep five kinds of breeding females.” So he kept cattle and sheep, and their increase was counted in tens of thousands. In Jiuzhen and Lujiang the people did not know how to plough with oxen and were often in want; Ren Yan and Wang Jing had farm tools cast and taught them to break new ground, which widened year by year until the people had enough. Dunhuang did not know how to make the seed drill; at sowing time the labour of men and oxen was spent and the grain harvested was still small. Huangfu Long taught them to make the seed drill: it saved more than half the labour, and the grain gained by half again. It was also the custom in Dunhuang for women to make skirts gathered up like a sheep’s gut, using a whole bolt of cloth; Long forbade it and had it changed, and again the saving was beyond counting. When Ci Chong was magistrate of Guiyang, the people did not plant mulberry and had none of the profit of silkworms or of weaving silk and hemp; most of them stuffed their clothes with the waste ends of hemp. They were idle and had few rough shoes; their feet split and bled, and in the depths of winter they all lit fires to warm them. Chong taught them to plant more mulberry and silkworm thorn, to raise silkworms and weave shoes, and had them plant ramie as well. Within a few years they were relying on it greatly, and their clothes and shoes were warm. That people south of the Yangzi now know mulberry, silkworms and the weaving of shoes is all due to Chong’s teaching. The soil of Wuyuan was right for hemp, but the people did not know how to spin and weave; in the winter months they had no clothes and lay in heaps of fine grass, and when they met an official they came out wearing the grass. Cui Shi made tools for spinning and weaving to teach them, and the people escaped the misery of cold. How can one say that teaching is not needed?

Li Heng built a house on the river islet at Longyang in Wuling and planted a thousand sweet orange trees. When he was dying he told his son: “In our district I have a thousand wooden servants. They will not ask you for food or clothing, and each will bring in a bolt of silk a year, which will be enough to live on.” By the end of Wu the oranges had matured, and they brought in several thousand bolts of silk a year. This is what the Grand Historian meant by “a thousand orange trees at Jiangling are equal to a marquis of a thousand households.” Fan Zhong, wanting to make vessels and furniture, first planted catalpa and lacquer trees, and people laughed at him. But as the years went by they all came into use, and those who had laughed came to borrow from him. So planting must never stop. The proverb says: “For a one-year plan, nothing is better than planting grain; for a ten-year plan, nothing is better than planting trees.” That is what this means.

Now I have gathered from the classics and commentaries, gone on to songs and sayings, asked those with long experience and tested it in practice; from ploughing and farming down to vinegar and pickles, every trade that supports life is written down, and I have called it Essential Arts for the Common People. There are ninety-two chapters, bound in ten books. Each book begins with a table of contents; it makes the text longer, but easier to look through. Grains, fruits and gourds that are not grown in the central lands are given by name only; how to plant them I have not learned. To leave the root for the branches is what the wise condemn; growing rich by the day and poor by the year is how hunger and cold creep up; so the business of merchants is left out and not recorded. Flowers and plants of the decorative kind may please the eye, but they have spring flowers and no autumn fruit; they are of a piece with empty show, and not worth keeping.

My purpose is to instruct the boys of my household; I do not presume to address the learned. So I have been careful and thorough, talking into their ears and pointing at each thing plainly, without valuing fine words. Let no reader laugh at it.
